Heat Pump Defrost Cycles Explained: What Is Normal, Timed vs. Demand Defrost, and What Field Data Shows

Every air-source heat pump frosts up in cold, damp weather and then melts that frost by briefly running in reverse. That is normal. What varies a lot is how often it happens, how long it lasts, and how much backup heat it calls for, and that comes down to the controls.

On a cold, damp morning, a homeowner looks outside and sees the heat pump’s fan stopped and a cloud of steam rising from the unit. It looks like a breakdown, but usually it’s a normal defrost cycle. Knowing what normal looks like helps you tell an everyday defrost from a real problem.

Who this helps: heat pump owners in their first winter, people comparing heat pumps for a cold climate, and anyone whose electric bill jumps in damp, near-freezing weather.

Why heat pumps defrost

In heating mode, the outdoor coil runs colder than the outside air. According to a national laboratory field validation study, frost forms when the coil surface is colder than the outdoor dew point. As it builds, it reduces airflow and heat transfer, so it has to be melted periodically. To do that, the heat pump switches into cooling mode and sends hot refrigerant to the outdoor coil.

A homeowner guide hosted by DOE says defrost typically takes 5 to 15 minutes. Fans may stop or slow, and melted water will drip from the outdoor unit, so make sure it can drain away and won’t refreeze on a walkway. On ducted systems, backup heat may come on during defrost to avoid a gap in heating.

When defrost happens most

Many people assume defrost is worst in the coldest weather. It isn’t. A 2026 study in Energy Efficiency followed 22 cold-climate heat pumps in occupied homes over two winters. Defrost was most frequent between 10°F and 30°F and at higher outdoor humidity. The median cycle lasted about 4 to 6 minutes, though some ran as long as 20. Very cold air holds less moisture, so frost forms more slowly. That means damp days just below freezing are the heaviest defrost days.

Timed vs. demand defrost

Control typeHow it decides to defrostStrengthsWeak spots
Timed (time-temperature)Fixed run-time interval when coil temperature is lowSimple and low cost; settings can be adjusted in the fieldCan defrost a clean coil, or wait too long on a heavy-frost day
Demand (sensor-based)Sensors infer frost from coil temperature, pressure, or performance dropDefrosts only when needed. Less sensitive to settingsDepends on sensors and software tuning

The difference can be large. A 2026 national laboratory simulation study modeled residential heat pumps across nine U.S. climate zones. It found that switching from timed to demand-based defrost could cut annual energy use by 11% to 38%. Timer results depended heavily on the chosen interval. Software matters too. In a DOE-funded test in Fairbanks, Alaska, the factory software defrosted about every 55 minutes even without significant frost. Including recovery, the unit spent about 60% of its time defrosting. An updated version cut defrost runtime by 65% to 85%.

The hidden cost is backup heat: in that study, defrost accounted for more than 20% of compressor heating energy at 4 of 12 sites, because electric backup heat ran during defrost. At 3 of 11 sites, defrost was the main reason backup heat ran at all. How backup heat is set up for defrost matters as much as the defrost control itself.

Comfort during defrost

The same Energy Efficiency study found that none of the defrost strategies changed average room temperature by more than about 1°F. But systems that ran the indoor fan during defrost without much backup heat often blew air below 60°F for a short time, which feels drafty near the registers. Dual-fuel systems handle this differently, since the furnace can carry heating during defrost.

Normal vs. call for service

  • Normal: steam or fog, dripping water, a whoosh as the reversing valve shifts, the outdoor fan pausing, and a few minutes of cooler air.
  • Call a technician: the coil stays covered in solid ice, or defrost never seems to end or happens back to back. Also call for ice building up from a clogged base pan or a blocked drain path, or if backup heat runs almost nonstop on mild days.
  • Setup items to ask about: defrost interval or mode settings, how backup heat is staged during defrost, the outdoor unit’s height above snow, and a clear path for meltwater to drain.

Limitations

Most findings here come from field studies of specific cold-climate units and from simulations, so your equipment may behave differently. The simulation’s 11% to 38% range is modeled, not measured, and not all models let installers change defrost settings. Always follow the manufacturer’s installation instructions for clearances, stands, and defrost settings.
