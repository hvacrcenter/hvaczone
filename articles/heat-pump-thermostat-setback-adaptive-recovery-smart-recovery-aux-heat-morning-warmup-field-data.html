Heat Pump Thermostat Setbacks: Adaptive Recovery, Aux Heat, and What Field Data Shows

Turning the heat down at night is classic energy advice. With a heat pump, the savings depend on how the house warms back up in the morning. New field data shows why, and which smart thermostat settings matter.

The familiar advice is to turn the thermostat down 7–10°F for eight hours a day. The Building America Solution Center repeats the U.S. Department of Energy estimate that this can save as much as 10% a year on heating and cooling. That works well for a furnace or boiler. A heat pump is different. When the house has cooled several degrees, the thermostat may call for electric resistance backup heat (often labeled “AUX”) to catch up quickly. Resistance heat delivers about one unit of heat per unit of electricity. A heat pump’s compressor delivers more, which is why every hour of strip heat cuts into the savings.

Who this helps: homeowners with heat pumps or dual-fuel systems, anyone who programs setbacks on a smart thermostat, and building managers who schedule unoccupied setbacks.

What new field data shows

A 57-site study of cold-climate variable-speed heat pumps in Washington, Oregon, Idaho, and Montana, presented at the 2026 ACEEE Summer Study, ran a controlled test at 14 homes. Participants used a 3–5°F, eight-hour daily setback in January and held a steady temperature in February. With setbacks, average power across the sites nearly doubled at 6 a.m. At two sites, recovery-hour power topped 10 kW, compared with about 2 kW for the same hours without a setback. The authors report the homes used about the same energy, or more, with a setback than without one.

A National Renewable Energy Laboratory field study of 12 ducted variable-speed heat pumps found the same pattern. At one site, backup heat came on after 6 a.m. during setback recovery even though it was above 40°F outside and the aux lockout was set at 35°F. NREL recommends gradual temperature changes, smart recovery features, and relying on the compressor whenever possible.

Closer to home, a New Jersey dual-fuel study by DNV and New Jersey Natural Gas inspected 16 hybrid systems. Seven had nighttime setbacks of at least 3°F, two were set back 8°F, and all recovered between 5 and 7 a.m., often the coldest part of the day. The two systems with adaptive recovery turned off had the 8°F and 4°F setbacks. The authors note that large setbacks with uncontrolled recovery make backup heating more likely.

How adaptive recovery works

According to the Building America Solution Center, heat pump thermostats with adaptive recovery learn how long the system takes to reach the setpoint and start the warmup earlier. The heat pump then raises the temperature gradually on its own instead of calling for strip heat. Within about a week, the thermostat adjusts to the home’s equipment, construction, and climate. This isn’t new: a 1982 ASHRAE paper found that better recovery control raised heat pump setback savings and substantially reduced peak electrical demand.

Key takeaway: with a heat pump, a setback saves only if the morning warmup runs on the compressor. If the AUX indicator lights up every morning, the setback is costing you money and adding to the grid’s winter morning peak.

Gas furnaces, boilers, and dual fuel

Setbacks still make sense with gas furnaces, which recover quickly. Radiant and high-mass systems recover slowly, so start the warmup earlier. With dual fuel, the switchover point and recovery settings work together. A deep setback can push recovery onto the furnace or strip heat instead of the heat pump.

Limitations

These field samples are small: 14, 12, and 16 sites. Results depend on equipment sizing, ducts, insulation, climate, and how each thermostat handles recovery. Some homes may save with a modest setback, others won’t. Your own aux runtime data is the best guide. Never disable backup heat a system needs for freeze protection or comfort in extreme cold.
