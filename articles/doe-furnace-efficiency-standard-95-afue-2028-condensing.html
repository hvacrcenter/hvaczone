The DOE 95% AFUE Furnace Rule: What Changes in 2028 and What It Means for Your Home

Starting December 18, 2028, every new residential gas furnace manufactured in the U.S. must achieve at least 95% AFUE — up from today's 80% minimum. The rule effectively ends non-condensing furnace production, requires PVC sidewall venting and condensate drainage, and faces a Senate bill that would void it entirely. Here's where things stand.

The U.S. Department of Energy finalized energy conservation standards for residential gas furnaces in December 2023, requiring non-weatherized gas furnaces and mobile home gas furnaces to achieve at least 95% annual fuel utilization efficiency (AFUE) — up from the current 80% minimum that has been in place since 2015. The compliance date is December 18, 2028, and the rule applies to the date of manufacture, with no sell-through or installation deadlines ([DOE, Consumer Furnaces](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/buildings/consumer-furnaces); [DOE, Finalized Standards](https://www.energy.gov/articles/doe-finalizes-energy-efficiency-standards-residential-furnaces-save-americans-15-billion)).

What 95% AFUE means

AFUE measures the percentage of fuel converted to usable heat over a heating season. An 80% AFUE furnace wastes 20 cents of every fuel dollar as hot exhaust up the flue. A 95% AFUE furnace captures nearly all of that waste heat using a secondary heat exchanger that condenses water vapor from the flue gas, extracting latent heat before the exhaust exits the building. DOE stated that "this standard is readily achievable by modern condensing furnaces, which use secondary heat exchangers to capture excess heat from furnace exhaust gases" ([DOE, Finalized Standards](https://www.energy.gov/articles/doe-finalizes-energy-efficiency-standards-residential-furnaces-save-americans-15-billion)).

The 95% level effectively requires Category IV condensing technology. Non-condensing furnaces — which vent through traditional B-vent or masonry chimneys using natural draft — cannot reach 95% AFUE. Condensing furnaces use sealed combustion with positive-pressure PVC sidewall or roof venting and produce acidic condensate that must be collected, neutralized, and drained ([ICC, Building Safety Journal](https://www.iccsafe.org/building-safety-journal/bsj-technical/doe-water-heater-and-furnace-efficiency-rules-drive-a-nationwide-shift-to-condensing-technologies/)).

Non-condensing furnaces currently constitute approximately 55% of the U.S. natural gas furnace market, according to the American Gas Association's petition to DOE ([DOE, AGA Petition](https://www.energy.gov/documents/aga-petition-rulemaking)).

Projected savings and environmental impact

DOE estimates the standards will reduce household utility costs by $1.5 billion annually and save consumers $24.8 billion on energy bills over 30 years. Over that same period, the standards are projected to cut carbon emissions by 332 million metric tons and methane emissions by 4.3 million tons. Residential gas furnaces accounted for approximately 19% of annual residential energy use in the United States as of 2022. The furnace standards were last updated in 2007 ([DOE, Finalized Standards](https://www.energy.gov/articles/doe-finalizes-energy-efficiency-standards-residential-furnaces-save-americans-15-billion)).

The retrofit challenge.Replacing a non-condensing furnace with a condensing model is not a simple swap. The existing B-vent or chimney must be abandoned or relined, new PVC sidewall venting must be routed, and a condensate drain line must be installed. In homes where the furnace shares a common vent with a water heater, removing the furnace can leave the water heater with an oversized vent, causing weak draft, backdraft, and code non-compliance. These retrofit costs are separate from the furnace itself ([ICC, Building Safety Journal](https://www.iccsafe.org/building-safety-journal/bsj-technical/doe-water-heater-and-furnace-efficiency-rules-drive-a-nationwide-shift-to-condensing-technologies/)).

Legal challenges and the current status

The rule has been contested since its proposal. Gas industry groups and manufacturers argued that the rule would force non-condensing furnaces out of the market — violating the Energy Policy and Conservation Act (EPCA), which prohibits DOE from imposing standards that eliminate a product class or performance characteristic. On November 4, 2025, the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals upheld the rule in a 2-1 decision. The court cited prior DOE analyses concluding that condensing furnaces "offer substantially the same performance characteristics and features as non-condensing options." Dissenting Judge Neomi Rao wrote that "the ability to vent through a traditional chimney is exactly the kind of real-world feature Congress protected from elimination in the marketplace" ([Home Pros News](https://homepros.news/95-efficiency-rule-for-gas-furnaces-stands-court-rules/)).

The American Gas Association petitioned DOE to delay the compliance dates for both the furnace rule and the commercial water heater rule until January 1, 2030, at a minimum, while Supreme Court review is pending. The petition states that manufacturers of covered products and associated venting equipment have "no choice now but to spend millions of dollars preparing to comply with the rules" and that these expenditures could become stranded if the rules are ultimately deemed unlawful ([DOE, AGA Petition](https://www.energy.gov/documents/aga-petition-rulemaking)).

The Senate bill that would void the rule

Utah Senator Mike Lee, chair of the Senate Energy and Natural Resources Committee, introduced the Energy Efficiency Reform Act of 2026, which would void the 95% residential gas furnace rule and amend EPCA to make future standards changes harder. The Appliance Standards Awareness Project estimated that the bill would raise household utility bills by $160 per year. The bill has been referred to the Senate Committee on Energy and Natural Resources and must undergo subcommittee hearings and markup before floor consideration ([Home Pros News](https://homepros.news/new-bill-would-cement-doe-appliance-standard-overhaul/)).

What homeowners should know now

Even if the rule survives all legal and legislative challenges, the compliance date of December 18, 2028 applies to manufacture — not installation. Existing non-condensing furnaces can be repaired, and any non-condensing units manufactured before that date can be installed afterward. There is no mandate to replace a functioning furnace. However, homeowners planning a replacement in the next 2-3 years should understand that condensing technology will likely be the only new option available by late 2028, and that installation may require venting and condensate drainage modifications.

Separately, ENERGY STAR Furnace Version 5.0 took effect on July 31, 2026, with regional criteria differing for the U.S. North, U.S. South, and Canada. Products manufactured or imported after that date must meet the updated specification to carry the ENERGY STAR label ([Sylvane](https://www.sylvane.com/blogs/knowledge-center/2026-energy-efficiency-rating-updates-what-s-changing-for-energy-star-and-appliances)).
