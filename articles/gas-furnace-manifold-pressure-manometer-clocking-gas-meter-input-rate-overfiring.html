Manometers and Clocking the Gas Meter: How Techs Verify a Furnace Is Firing at the Right Rate

A furnace’s rating plate says how much gas it should burn. A manometer and a stopwatch at the gas meter tell you how much it actually burns. Here is how the two checks work together, and why a federal lab test showed that pressure alone can miss by 10%.

Every gas furnace has a rating plate that lists its input in Btu per hour, which is how much gas it is designed to burn. Whether it actually burns that amount depends on gas pressure, orifice size, and the heat content of the gas your utility delivers. Two simple tools check this: a manometer and a stopwatch.

Who this helps: homeowners who want to know what a proper furnace startup or tune-up should include, and technicians who want to review the method.

Tool 1: the manometer

A manometer measures gas pressure in inches of water column (w.c.). Techs connect it to the inlet tap ahead of the gas valve to read supply pressure, and to the outlet (manifold) tap after the valve to read the pressure feeding the burners. According to ACHR News, typical natural gas manifold readings fall around 3.2 to 3.8 inches w.c. The correct value is the one on the furnace’s data plate, and the reading depends on which stage the valve is firing. The same article notes that too much or too little gas can cause carbon monoxide, and that a furnace tripping its high limit can be a sign the gas valve was never set up correctly.

Tool 2: the gas meter and a stopwatch

Pressure alone doesn’t prove the firing rate. Clocking the meter does. As HVAC School explains, you shut off every other gas appliance and pilot, run the furnace at high fire until it settles, then time one full revolution of the meter’s smallest test dial. Then:

  • Cubic feet per hour (CFH) = 3,600 × dial size (cubic feet) ÷ seconds per revolution
  • Actual input (Btu/h) = CFH × heat content of the gas (Btu per cubic foot)

For example, a ½-cubic-foot dial that turns once every 22 seconds gives about 81.8 CFH. At 1,000 Btu per cubic foot, that’s roughly 81,800 Btu/h. HVAC School recommends checking manifold pressure and orifice size if the result is off from the rating plate by more than a couple of percent. It also notes that meters supplied at 2 psi need a pressure correction, or they will read about 14% low.

Why the heat content matters

Natural gas isn’t exactly 1,000 Btu per cubic foot. The U.S. Energy Information Administration says the 2023 U.S. average for gas delivered to customers was about 1,038 Btu per cubic foot, and that it varies by location and over time. Some utilities print the number on the bill. Using the right figure changes the result by a few percent, about the same size as the tolerance techs are trying to hit.

What a federal lab test found

In a furnace test by the Consumer Product Safety Commission, the installation instructions told the installer to clock the meter and to set manifold pressure to 3.5 inches w.c., without saying which step takes priority. When staff set the pressure to 3.5 inches, the 100,000 Btu/h furnace actually fired at about 110,000 Btu/h, roughly 10% over. With an intact vent, CO stayed under 3 ppm. With a fully blocked vent, the highest CO measured was 6 ppm at the rated input and 331 ppm at 110,000 Btu/h. The same CPSC report also noted that the flame looked the same even at 23% overfire, so you can’t judge firing rate by eye.

Key takeaway: the manometer sets the pressure, and clocking the meter confirms the input. A blue flame and a “normal” pressure reading don’t prove a furnace is firing at its rated input. Pair these checks with combustion analysis and CO testing.

Limitations

Clocking only works when no other gas appliance is drawing from the meter, and it isn’t practical on some commercial or electronic meters. High altitude and propane systems have their own pressure and orifice requirements. Gas valve adjustments should be made only by a qualified technician following the installation instructions and local fuel gas code. This is not a do-it-yourself task.
