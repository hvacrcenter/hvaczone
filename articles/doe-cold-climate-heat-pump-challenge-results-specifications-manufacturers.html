DOE Cold Climate Heat Pump Challenge: Results, Specs, and What It Means for Northern HVAC

The DOE challenged 8 manufacturers to build heat pumps that deliver 100% heating capacity at 5°F with a COP above 2.4 and refrigerant GWP under 750. All prototypes passed. Field testing across 22 sites in the northern U.S. and Canada confirmed reliable operation to -15°F — and commercial products are now arriving.

Conventional air-source heat pumps lose 30-60% of their heating capacity when outdoor temperatures drop below freezing, often falling back on expensive electric resistance backup heat. In 2021, DOE launched the Residential Cold Climate Heat Pump (CCHP) Challenge to change that — pushing manufacturers to build systems that maintain full heating capacity at 5°F while using low-GWP refrigerants and supporting grid demand response. The results are now in, and commercial products are reaching the market ([ACEEE](https://www.aceee.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/rising-up-to-the-challenge-cold-climate.pdf), [DOE](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/buildings/heating-ventilation-and-air-conditioning-rd-project-portfolio)).

The challenge specifications

DOE's specification, developed through workshops with industry partners starting in 2021, requires participating heat pumps to meet demanding cold-climate performance targets. At 5°F (-15°C), systems must deliver 100% of their rated heating capacity (compared to capacity at 47°F) and achieve a COP of at least 2.4 for systems rated 24,000-48,000 Btu/h, or 2.1 for larger systems. The compressor must start at temperatures at or below -5°F and continue operating down to -10°F. An optional extreme-cold target extends operation to -15°F with compressor cut-out at -20°F. Refrigerant GWP must not exceed 750 on a 100-year basis — ruling out R-410A (GWP 2,088) and requiring low-GWP alternatives like R-32 or R-454B. All systems must meet HSPF2 of 8.5 in Region V, maintain a turndown ratio of at least 30% at 47°F, and comply with ENERGY STAR demand-response requirements including general curtailment (≤70% rated power) and critical curtailment (≤40% rated power) ([ACEEE](https://www.aceee.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/rising-up-to-the-challenge-cold-climate.pdf)).

Eight manufacturers, two rounds, 22 field sites

Eight manufacturers committed to the challenge: Bosch, Carrier, Daikin, Johnson Controls, Lennox, Midea, Rheem, and Trane. The first group (Lennox, Carrier, Trane, Rheem) completed laboratory testing in 2022 and began field testing in winter 2022-2023. The second group (Daikin, Midea, Bosch, Johnson Controls) completed lab testing in 2023 and started field testing in winter 2023-2024. Field validation covered 22 sites: 19 occupied single-family homes across the northern United States and 3 test homes at Natural Resources Canada in Ottawa. Data was collected at 1-second intervals for outdoor-unit and indoor-unit power, with outdoor temperature logged every 5 minutes. At least one year of data was captured for each site ([ACEEE](https://www.aceee.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/rising-up-to-the-challenge-cold-climate.pdf)).

Results: all prototypes exceeded every target

In laboratory testing, all prototypes provided at least 100% of nominal rated heating capacity at 5°F — the core challenge requirement. All exceeded the COP target (2.1 or 2.4 depending on capacity class) at 5°F, making them more than twice as efficient as electric resistance heating (COP of 1.0). Several prototypes demonstrated heating operation at -15°F, meeting the optional extreme-cold target. All prototypes exceeded federal minimum standards (14.3 SEER2 cooling, 8.5 HSPF2 Region V heating) and demonstrated compliance with connected-product demand-response requirements ([ACEEE](https://www.aceee.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/rising-up-to-the-challenge-cold-climate.pdf)).

In field testing, heating-season COPs ranged from 1.8 at -15°F to 3.5 at 45-55°F for compressor-only operation. Auxiliary electric heat contribution varied widely at very cold temperatures — from nearly 0% to as much as 90% — but generally decreased above 0°F as the heat pump handled more of the load. Homeowners reported improved comfort and satisfaction compared to their previous gas or electric furnace systems, with some noting increased airflow noise at very low temperatures due to higher CCHP airflow rates compared to furnaces ([ACEEE](https://www.aceee.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/rising-up-to-the-challenge-cold-climate.pdf)).

The capacity revolution.Conventional heat pumps typically deliver only 40-70% of rated heating capacity at 5°F, with market-available cold-climate units averaging COPs of 1.75-2.25. The DOE Challenge required 100% capacity and COP above 2.4 — and every prototype met it. This means cold-climate heat pumps can now replace furnaces without relying on electric resistance backup, a breakthrough for residential electrification in northern states ([ACEEE](https://www.aceee.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/rising-up-to-the-challenge-cold-climate.pdf)).

Commercial products arriving now

Trane introduced residential cold-climate heat pumps under the Trane and American Standard brands that maintain 100% heating capacity down to 5°F and continue operating to -20°F. The systems were validated through nearly 18,000 hours of field testing in Minnesota, Montana, New York, Pennsylvania, Wisconsin, and Texas, with outdoor temperatures reaching -21°F. Trane's prototype operated reliably to -23°F and delivered approximately 15% energy savings over the field trial period ([Trane/Las Vegas Sun](https://lasvegassun.com/news/2026/oct/01/trane-technologies-introduces-residential-cold-climate/)).

On the commercial side, DOE's Commercial Building HVAC Technology Challenge is running in parallel. Rheem completed lab validation for a 10-14 ton rooftop heat pump that delivered 110% heating capacity at 5°F and 90% at -10°F, exceeding DOE efficiency benchmarks by 55% (IVEC), 20% (IVHEc), and 25% (COP2 at -10°F) ([Contracting Business](https://www.contractingbusiness.com/industry-news/news/55363755/rheem-rooftop-heat-pump-surpasses-doe-cold-climate-hvac-challenge-targets)). LG also completed lab validation for a 10-ton heat pump rooftop unit, now in field trials at a retail store in Indiana and at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory in Washington, with testing continuing through 2027 ([LG/PRNewswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/lg-advances-in-us-department-of-energys-commercial-building-hvac-technology-challenge-302763832.html)).

What this means for homeowners and contractors

  • Furnace replacement: Cold-climate heat pumps can now replace furnaces in northern states without backup heat in most conditions — a major shift from even five years ago.
  • Low-GWP refrigerant: The GWP ≤750 requirement means these systems use R-32 or R-454B, not R-410A — contractors need A2L training and compatible tools.
  • Grid-interactive: All challenge systems support demand response, enabling utility programs that pay homeowners to reduce HVAC load during peak periods.
  • Sizing matters: Even with 100% capacity at 5°F, proper Manual J load calculation remains essential — oversizing wastes efficiency, undersizing risks comfort at design temperature.
  • Auxiliary heat strategy: Field data shows auxiliary heat use varies from 0% to 90% at very cold temperatures — thermostat setup and switchover settings dramatically affect operating costs.
  • Commercial applications: Rooftop heat pump units delivering 110% capacity at 5°F open electrification opportunities for commercial buildings in cold climates.
