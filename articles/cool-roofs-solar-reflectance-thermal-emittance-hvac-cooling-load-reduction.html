Cool Roofs and HVAC: Solar Reflectance, Thermal Emittance, and Cooling Load Reduction

A conventional dark roof can reach 150-190°F on a sunny day. A cool roof stays 50-60°F cooler by reflecting sunlight and radiating heat — and the HVAC system reaps the benefit. EPA research shows cool roofs reduce cooling loads 18-93% and peak cooling demand 11-27%, while DOE reports up to 15% annual air-conditioning energy savings for single-story buildings.

The roof is the most sun-exposed surface on any building, and its properties directly determine how much heat reaches the HVAC system. A cool roof uses two physical properties — solar reflectance and thermal emittance — to stay dramatically cooler than a conventional roof, reducing the cooling load that the HVAC system must handle. DOE's Guide to Cool Roofs defines solar reflectance as the fraction of sunlight reflected (0 to 1 scale) and thermal emittance as the efficiency with which a surface cools itself by emitting thermal radiation (0 to 1 scale) (DOE).

The numbers: how much does a cool roof save?

An EPA-commissioned study published in Energy and Buildings found that increasing roof solar reflectance by 0.65 (from a conventional 0.20 to a cool 0.85) reduced cooling loads by 18-93% and peak cooling demand by 11-27%, depending on climate and building type. Annual cooling-load savings ranged from 19-65% across five representative climates. The study also documented heating penalties of 0.2-17 kWh/m² per year — small compared to cooling savings in warm climates, but a factor in cold regions (EPA).

DOE reports that in warm or hot climates, substituting a cool roof for a conventional one can reduce annual air-conditioning energy use of a single-story building by up to 15%. EPA adds that cool roofs reduce peak cooling demand by 11-27% in air-conditioned residential buildings, and in non-air-conditioned buildings they lower maximum indoor temperatures by 1.2-3.3°C (2.2-5.9°F) (DOE, EPA).

What the codes require

ASHRAE Standard 90.1-2022 Section 5.5.3.1.4 requires cool roof performance for most commercial buildings, specifying minimum three-year-aged solar reflectance and thermal emittance values. The IECC 2024 Section C402.4 extends cool roof requirements to low-sloped roofs in Climate Zones 0-3. Jurisdictions that have adopted these requirements typically specify a minimum three-year-aged solar reflectance of 0.55, thermal emittance of 0.75, or alternatively a Solar Reflectance Index (SRI) of 64. Florida's Building Code 2023 requires SR 0.63 and SRI 75 for Climate Zone 1A — the most stringent in the nation (Cool Roof Rating Council).

Aged vs. initial reflectance.DOE's guidance is clear: energy savings should be based on the roof's aged solar reflectance, not its initial value, because many roofs become less reflective over time. A white roof that starts at SR 0.85 may degrade to 0.65 after three years of weathering. Code requirements specify three-year-aged values for this reason. When evaluating cool roof payback, use aged values — not the showroom number (DOE).

Cost and payback

DOE estimates annual energy savings of up to $0.20 per square foot for a white roof on a commercial building, and up to $0.05 per square foot for a cool-color roof on a home. The additional cost for cool asphalt shingles compared to conventional shingles is up to $0.50 per square foot, while reflective surface coatings cost about $1 per square foot. For a 10,000 sq ft commercial roof in a hot climate saving $0.20/sq ft/year, the annual savings reach $2,000 — and coatings can pay back in under five years (DOE).

Climate considerations and the heating penalty

Cool roofs are most beneficial in hot and warm climates. In mixed climates, winter heating penalties can occur because the roof reflects sunlight that would otherwise warm the building. The EPA study found heating penalties of 0.2-17 kWh/m² per year — but these are typically much smaller than summer cooling savings. In cold climates (Climate Zones 5-7), the heating penalty can approach or exceed the cooling benefit, making cool roofs less cost-effective. NREL's Cool Roof Resource Guide recommends evaluating both cooling savings and heating penalties using location-specific data before specifying a cool roof in mixed or cold climates (NREL).

What this means for HVAC system design

  • Load calculations: ACCA Manual J and ASHRAE load calculations should account for roof solar reflectance — a cool roof can reduce the roof heat gain component of the cooling load by 50% or more.
  • Equipment sizing: Buildings with cool roofs may need smaller cooling equipment, reducing first cost as well as operating cost.
  • Peak demand: The 11-27% peak cooling demand reduction can lower demand charges on commercial electric bills — often the largest component of summer utility costs.
  • Retrofit opportunity: Applying a reflective coating to an existing dark roof is one of the lowest-cost HVAC load reduction strategies available.
  • Roof longevity: Cool roofs experience less thermal cycling stress, potentially extending roof life and reducing maintenance costs.
