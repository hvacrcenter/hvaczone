Portable HEPA Air Cleaners: CADR Sizing, Real-Home Results, and How They Work With Your HVAC

With windows closed for winter and holiday cooking ramping up, indoor particles build up. A portable HEPA air cleaner can help, but only if it’s sized right and actually runs. Here is what the ratings mean and what studies in real homes found.

A central HVAC filter only works while the blower runs. The U.S. EPA’s Guide to Air Cleaners in the Home notes that most systems run less than 25% of the time during heating and cooling seasons. A portable air cleaner filters one room continuously, whether or not the furnace is on. That makes it a useful complement in bedrooms, nurseries, home offices, and apartments where the central filter can’t be upgraded.

Who this helps: households with asthma or allergies, anyone near busy roads or cooking-heavy kitchens, renters without control of the central system, and homeowners deciding between a portable unit and a filter upgrade.

Sizing with CADR

Clean Air Delivery Rate (CADR) is measured in cubic feet per minute. Many labels list three numbers: smoke, dust, and pollen. EPA says to use the smoke CADR for the fine particles (PM2.5) of greatest health concern, and its rule of thumb is a smoke CADR of at least two-thirds of the room’s floor area. For a 300 sq ft room, that’s 195 cfm. The Washington State Department of Health gives the same two-thirds guidance. For higher ceilings or open floor plans, size up or use more than one unit.

The ENERGY STAR room air cleaner specification sets minimum efficiency by smoke CADR per watt: 1.9 for units rated 30–100 CADR, 2.4 for 100–150, and 2.9 at 150 and above. ENERGY STAR also notes that larger models use more energy, so it pays to match the unit to the room.

What real-home studies found

Lab ratings are one thing; occupied homes are another. A 2026 Environment International study of 19 Boston-area homes compared five days with and five days without a portable HEPA cleaner in the family room. Average PM2.5 fell from 5.9 to 2.2 µg/m³, about a 60% reduction. Basements saw a smaller drop than the rooms with the units. The authors also found reductions in particle-bound radioactivity from radon decay products. They caution that HEPA filtration does not remove radon gas itself, so it is no substitute for radon mitigation.

A tougher test came during the January 2025 Eaton Fire. In a npj Clean Air analysis of 27 Los Angeles homes in randomized trials, homes running HEPA purifiers had indoor PM2.5 about 15% lower than homes with sham units, which had the filter removed. Indoor levels still rose in both groups. The authors concluded portable HEPA units helped but weren’t enough alone during heavy smoke; building and behavior measures still matter.

Key takeaway: a properly sized HEPA unit running continuously in the room you use most can cut fine particles substantially. It works best alongside source control, good ventilation, and the best central filter your system can handle.

Portable unit or central filter?

EPA recommends at least a MERV 13 central filter, or the highest rating your system fan and filter slot can handle, and says a technician may be needed to confirm what will work. A central upgrade covers the whole house, but only while the blower runs. Running the fan longer costs electricity, especially with older PSC motors. A portable unit runs constantly in one room. Many homes benefit from both: a better central filter plus a portable HEPA unit in the bedroom.

Limitations

The Boston and Los Angeles studies are small, and results depend on room size, door positions, how often units run, and outdoor conditions. EPA notes that health improvements in studies are typically small and not always noticeable to the individual. Air cleaners don’t fix moisture, combustion, or radon problems at their source.
