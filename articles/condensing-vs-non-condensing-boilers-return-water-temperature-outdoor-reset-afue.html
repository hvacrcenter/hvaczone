Condensing vs. Non-Condensing Boilers: Why Return Water Temperature Decides Your Real Efficiency

A condensing boiler can be rated 95% AFUE and still run like an 85% boiler. The difference is the temperature of the water coming back to it. Here is how the ratings, the federal rules and the piping actually fit together, and what to check before a boiler replacement.

Many homes in North Jersey still heat with hot water: cast-iron radiators, baseboard, or radiant floors fed by a boiler. When that boiler needs replacing, the first question is usually condensing or non-condensing. The efficiency rating on the box is only half the answer. The other half is how your heating system is piped and controlled.

What the rules and labels say

Under current federal rules, residential boilers made since January 15, 2021 must meet these minimum AFUE ratings: 84% for gas hot-water, 82% for gas steam, 86% for oil hot-water and 85% for oil steam (10 CFR 430.32). Gas and oil hot-water boilers must also have an "automatic means for adjusting water temperature," and gas boilers can't use a constant-burning pilot (10 CFR 430.32). A 2023 proposal to raise boiler minimums, which would effectively have required condensing gas boilers, was withdrawn in January 2025 (DOE).

ENERGY STAR certifies gas boilers at 90% AFUE or higher and oil boilers at 87% or higher (ENERGY STAR). In practice, gas boilers at or above 90% are condensing designs.

Why return water temperature matters

A condensing boiler gets its extra efficiency by cooling the exhaust enough that the water vapor in it condenses and gives up its heat. That only happens when the water returning to the boiler is cool enough. The General Services Administration's field work on federal buildings put it plainly: efficiency "hinges on return water temperature below 130°F" (GSA). DOE's own boiler analysis notes that condensing boilers "need a low return water temperature" to operate as designed, and that at high return temperatures the "efficiency advantage" can be lost (DOE boiler analysis (Federal Register)). Commenters in that same proceeding estimated a condensing boiler stuck above 130°F return would run around 85% to 88% (DOE boiler analysis (Federal Register)).

When the conditions are right, the savings are real. In GSA's tests, condensing plants saved an average of 14% in Atlanta and 24% in Denver, with 4 to 6 year paybacks when installed as end-of-life replacements (GSA).

Field note: outdoor reset is the key. Most of the winter, a house doesn't need 180°F water. Outdoor reset lowers the supply temperature as the weather warms, which also lowers the return temperature, so the boiler spends more hours condensing. GSA lists outdoor reset on supply water as a best practice (GSA). A condensing boiler with reset turned off, or set flat at 180°F, is the most common reason we see real-world savings fall short.

Checklist before replacing a boiler

  • Heat-loss calculation, not nameplate matching. Many old boilers are oversized. GSA recommends a load calculation sized to the maximum load with no excess capacity (GSA).
  • Radiator inventory. The amount of radiation in the home determines how low the water temperature can go on a cold day.
  • Outdoor reset set up and verified. Make sure it's actually on, not left at the factory setting.
  • Piping and flow. Primary/secondary piping or low-loss headers, pump speed and flow rate all affect return temperature (GSA).
  • Venting and condensate. Confirm vent material, where the vent exits the house, and where the condensate goes.
  • Steam systems. Condensing boilers aren't used for steam. Steam homes need a different conversation.

Limitations

AFUE is a lab rating. Real-world efficiency depends on the radiation in the house, the controls, cycling and how well the system is installed. The GSA results are from large commercial buildings, not single homes, and should be read as direction, not a promise of savings for your house.
