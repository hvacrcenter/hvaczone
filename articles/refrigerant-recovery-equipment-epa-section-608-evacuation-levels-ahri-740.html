Refrigerant Recovery Equipment: EPA Section 608 Requirements, Evacuation Levels, and AHRI 740 Standards

Federal law prohibits venting any refrigerant into the atmosphere during HVAC service. EPA Section 608 mandates specific evacuation vacuum levels by appliance type and charge size, requires recovery equipment certified to AHRI Standard 740, and demands 80-90% refrigerant recovery from small appliances. Here's what the regulations require and how the equipment is rated.

Since 1993, Section 608 of the Clean Air Act (40 CFR Part 82, Subpart F) has made it a federal offense to knowingly vent refrigerant into the atmosphere during the service, maintenance, repair, or disposal of air-conditioning and refrigeration equipment. The prohibition applies not only to ozone-depleting substances like CFCs and HCFCs but also to their substitutes, including HFCs ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2018-09/documents/section_608_of_the_clean_air_act.pdf)). Every HVAC technician must understand the recovery, evacuation, and equipment certification requirements that enforce this prohibition.

Recover, recycle, reclaim: three distinct processes

EPA defines three categories of refrigerant handling, each with different purity requirements. To recover means to remove refrigerant in any condition from an appliance and store it in an external container without necessarily testing or processing it. To recycle means to extract refrigerant and clean it for reuse in the same owner's equipment — typically through oil separation and filter-driers that reduce moisture, acidity, and particulates. To reclaim means to reprocess recovered refrigerant to meet all specifications in AHRI Standard 700-2016, verified by analytical testing — a process that can only be performed by an EPA-certified reclaimer ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/section608/recovering-recycling-and-reclaiming-refrigerants)).

Required evacuation levels by appliance type

Before opening any refrigeration or air-conditioning system for service, technicians must evacuate the equipment (or isolated components) to EPA-specified vacuum levels. The requirements differ by pressure class, charge size, and whether the recovery equipment was manufactured before or after November 15, 1993. For post-1993 equipment, high-pressure appliances with charges under 200 pounds require evacuation to 0 inches Hg vacuum, while those with 200+ pound charges require 10 inches Hg vacuum. Medium-pressure appliances with 200+ pound charges require 15 inches Hg vacuum. Low-pressure appliances require evacuation to 25 mm Hg absolute — the most stringent standard, reflecting the difficulty of removing low-pressure refrigerants ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/section608/stationary-refrigeration-service-practice-requirements)).

Small appliance alternative.For small appliances (5 lbs or less of refrigerant — household refrigerators, window units, water coolers), EPA allows two compliance paths: recover at least 90% of the refrigerant (80% if the compressor is non-functional or the equipment was manufactured before November 15, 1993), or evacuate the appliance to 4 inches of mercury vacuum. The choice depends on equipment condition and recovery machine capability ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/section608/stationary-refrigeration-service-practice-requirements)).

AHRI Standard 740: how recovery equipment is rated

Recovery equipment must be certified by an EPA-approved organization — either AHRI or UL — to meet minimum performance requirements. The certification is based on AHRI Standard 740, which establishes definitions, test methods, and rating conditions for refrigerant recovery and recovery/recycling equipment. The standard covers four equipment types: self-contained equipment (operates independently of the system being serviced), system-dependent equipment (requires the system's compressor for recovery), recovery equipment, and recovery/recycling equipment ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/section608/refrigerant-recovery-and-recycling-equipment-certification), [AHRI](https://www.ahrinet.org/system/files/2024-09/AHRI%20Standard%20740-2016%20ICS%20code.pdf)).

AHRI 740 defines key performance metrics including liquid refrigerant recovery rate (measured by weight change over time in kg/min), vapor recovery rate (average withdrawal rate between two pressures), and push/pull liquid recovery (transferring liquid by creating pressure differentials between the system and receiving vessel). Certified equipment carries a label stating: "This equipment has been certified by AHRI/UL to meet EPA's minimum requirements for recycling and/or recovery equipment intended for use with [appropriate category of appliance]" ([AHRI](https://www.ahrinet.org/system/files/2024-09/AHRI%20Standard%20740-2016%20ICS%20code.pdf)).

Exceptions and special circumstances

EPA allows limited exceptions to the evacuation requirements. If technicians cannot reach specified vacuum levels because of system leaks, or because evacuation would substantially contaminate the refrigerant, they must isolate leaking from non-leaking components, evacuate non-leaking components to the full specified level, and evacuate leaking components to the lowest attainable level without contaminating the refrigerant — but never exceeding 0 psig. A second exception covers non-major repairs that are not followed by evacuation to the environment ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/section608/stationary-refrigeration-service-practice-requirements)).

Enforcement and penalties

EPA is authorized to assess civil penalties for any violation of Section 608 regulations. The agency performs random inspections, responds to tips from the public, and pursues cases against violators. Section 608 requirements apply to all refrigerants — not just ozone-depleting substances — and include technician certification (passing an EPA-approved examination), leak repair requirements for systems with 50+ pounds of refrigerant, and recordkeeping obligations. The final person in the disposal chain must ensure refrigerants are removed before appliance disposal ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2018-09/documents/section_608_of_the_clean_air_act.pdf)).

Choosing recovery equipment: what to look for

  • AHRI 740 certification: Look for the AHRI or UL certification label — it is legally required for equipment manufactured after November 15, 1993.
  • Recovery rate: Compare liquid and vapor recovery rates (kg/min) at AHRI 740 test conditions. Faster recovery reduces service time.
  • Refrigerant compatibility: Verify the equipment is rated for the refrigerants you service, including A2L flammable refrigerants (Appendix B4 requirements).
  • Self-contained vs. system-dependent: Self-contained units are more versatile for field service; system-dependent units may be adequate for shop work.
  • Push/pull capability: Essential for large commercial systems with significant liquid refrigerant charges.
  • Calibration: Pressure gauges should be calibrated to NIST-traceable standards. NIST piston gauge standards provide pressure calibration with expanded uncertainties of 8-40 ppm ([NIST](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication250-39e2009.pdf)).
