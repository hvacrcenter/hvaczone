Refrigerant Charging by Superheat, Subcooling, and Weight: The Tools, the Targets, and How Often Charge Is Wrong

A new air conditioner or heat pump ships with a factory charge sized for a short line set. Getting the final charge right takes a scale, accurate pressure and temperature readings, and the manufacturer’s target. Field studies show it is skipped or done wrong more often than most homeowners would guess.

Split air conditioners and heat pumps leave the factory with refrigerant already inside. According to a 2026 ACEEE paper from Pacific Northwest National Laboratory and Proctor Engineering Group, that charge is typically sized for a line set of about 15 to 25 feet. Longer lines, a different indoor coil, or a leak all change what the system actually needs. The installer is supposed to confirm the final charge with measurements, not guesswork.

Who this helps: homeowners who want to know what a proper charge check looks like, and technicians tightening up their charging routine.

The three methods

Weigh-in: recover or start from a known amount and add refrigerant by weight on a digital scale, using the manufacturer’s per-foot adjustment for the line set. It works in any weather and is the starting point for every new install.

Superheat: used on fixed-orifice (piston) systems. The ENERGY STAR National HVAC Commissioning Checklist defines it as the suction line temperature minus the evaporator saturation temperature from the suction pressure. The target comes from the manufacturer’s superheat table, which uses the outdoor temperature and the return air reading.

Subcooling: used on TXV and electronic expansion valve systems. It is the condenser saturation temperature from the liquid line pressure minus the actual liquid line temperature. The target is usually printed on the outdoor unit’s data plate or in the installation manual.

The tolerances

The ENERGY STAR HVAC Quality Installation guide, which is based on ACCA Standard 5 (HVAC Quality Installation), requires charge to be checked with subcooling on TXV systems, superheat on fixed-orifice systems, or the method the manufacturer requires. Results must land within ±3°F of the subcooling goal or ±5°F of the superheat goal. The commissioning checklist adds that the system should run 15 minutes before testing.

How often charge is wrong

  • A 2004 ACEEE field study of 4,168 air conditioners found about 72% outside manufacturer tolerances for charge, and 44% had improper airflow. Undercharged non-TXV units needed charge additions averaging 16.7% of the factory charge.
  • The 2026 ACEEE paper tested 83 homes with systems installed in the past year. In homes from quality-installation programs, 92% were charged correctly. In homes done to standard regional practice, only 55% were. Most of the incorrect systems (77%) were undercharged.
  • A 2024 DOE Building America report by Southface Energy Institute found 76.9% of 2,265 systems commissioned with an app-based diagnostic tool had correct charge.
  • The U.S. Department of Energy literature review found that across studies, 29 to 78% of systems were undercharged and 4 to 50% overcharged, depending on region and method.

What it costs

In simulations of a split air conditioner, NIST found annual energy use rose about 3%, 8%, and 16% for undercharges of 10%, 20%, and 30%. Using results from NIST Technical Note 1848, a national-lab analysis reports that 20% and 30% overcharges raised heat pump energy use by about 4 to 7% and 10 to 14%. NIST named refrigerant undercharge one of the most significant installation faults, after duct leakage. Systems with a TXV tolerate low charge better: the U.S. Department of Energy literature review notes their capacity and efficiency hold up until charge falls to around 70%.

Charging in November: superheat and subcooling readings need warm outdoor conditions. The ENERGY STAR HVAC Quality Installation guide says the outdoor temperature typically must be at least 55°F for superheat testing and 60°F for subcooling. In cold weather, weigh in the charge and follow the manufacturer’s cold-weather or heating-mode procedure, then recheck in spring.

New refrigerants: read the right column

R-454B, the most common R-410A replacement in new equipment, is a blend with a temperature glide of about 2°F, according to the refrigerant maker’s product data sheet. Its pressure-temperature guide lists separate saturated-vapor values for calculating superheat and saturated-liquid values for subcooling. Using one column for both builds in an error. Most digital gauges handle this when the correct refrigerant is selected.

Limitations

Superheat and subcooling only tell you something useful when the indoor airflow is right and the coils are clean. Low airflow can make a correctly charged system look overcharged or undercharged. Many of the field studies are from the Southwest, Florida, and California, so the exact percentages may differ in New Jersey. Ductless and variable-speed systems often use their own manufacturer procedures, which take priority over generic targets.
