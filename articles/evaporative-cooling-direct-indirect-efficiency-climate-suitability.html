Evaporative Cooling: Direct vs Indirect Systems, Efficiency, and Climate Suitability

Evaporative coolers use one-fourth the electricity of central air conditioners and achieve coefficients of performance of 9 to 20. But they only work where the air is dry. ASHRAE, DOE, and USGS data map exactly where these systems shine — and where they fail.

Evaporative cooling is the oldest mechanical cooling method — ancient Egyptians hung wet reeds in windows, and Persians built wind towers over water cisterns. Today's evaporative coolers, also called swamp coolers, use the same physics: water evaporation absorbs heat from air. What makes this technology compelling is its energy efficiency. The U.S. Department of Energy reports that evaporative coolers use approximately one-fourth as much energy as central air conditioners and cost less than half as much to install (DOE Building America).

How evaporative cooling works

When unsaturated air passes through a wetted media pad, water evaporates into the air. The latent heat of vaporization is drawn from the air's sensible heat, lowering the dry-bulb temperature while raising the humidity. The maximum possible cooling is the wet-bulb depression — the difference between the dry-bulb and wet-bulb temperatures. Cooling effectiveness measures how close the supply air gets to the wet-bulb temperature: a 90% effective cooler delivering air from 105°F dry-bulb / 60°F wet-bulb conditions produces 64.5°F supply air (DOE Building America).

ASHRAE's Handbook of HVAC Systems and Equipment reports that the coefficient of performance for indirect evaporative cooling typically ranges from 9 to 20, depending on the available dry-bulb temperature depression. This is 3 to 7 times higher than a high-efficiency vapor-compression air conditioner operating at COP 3 (ASHRAE Handbook).

Direct systems are the simplest and cheapest but add moisture to the supply air, which limits comfort in already-humid conditions. Indirect systems use a heat exchanger so the supply air is cooled without gaining humidity — ideal for commercial buildings, data centers, and applications requiring 100% outdoor air. Two-stage systems combine indirect pre-cooling with direct final cooling, achieving effectiveness above 100% (because the indirect stage lowers the incoming wet-bulb temperature before the direct stage operates). A peer-reviewed review found two-stage systems deliver COP around 14 with power savings up to 60% compared to mechanical vapor-compression systems (Wiley).

The climate catch

Evaporative cooling only works where the air is dry enough to absorb meaningful moisture. The DOE's Building America Solution Center identifies the key threshold: evaporative coolers work best in dry climates where the 1% summer design wet-bulb temperature is below 70°F (Region A), and can provide cooling where coincidental wet-bulb temperatures reach 74°F but with reduced performance (Region B) (DOE Building America).

The USGS maps this geographically: evaporative coolers work best in the dry areas of the western United States — Arizona, Nevada, New Mexico, Colorado, Utah, and parts of California, Oregon, Washington, Idaho, and Montana — and are not effective in the humid eastern United States, where conventional air conditioning is required (USGS).

The water-energy trade-off.A University of Arizona study quantified the trade-off for a 2,000-square-foot Tucson home: a swamp cooler used 250 kWh/month ($25) versus an air conditioner's 850 kWh/month ($85). But the swamp cooler consumed 4,620 gallons of water per month directly, while the air conditioner used zero direct water (though its electricity generation consumed 425 gallons off-site under coal power). In drought-stressed regions, water consumption is the limiting factor for evaporative cooling adoption (University of Arizona CLIMAS).

Where evaporative cooling excels

  • Data centers and server rooms: Indirect evaporative cooling can provide free cooling for thousands of hours per year in dry climates, dramatically reducing compressor runtime.
  • Warehouses and industrial buildings: High ceilings and large volumes benefit from the low operating cost and high airflow of direct systems.
  • Residential in arid climates: A modern 85% effective cooler can reduce 100°F intake air to approximately 68°F supply air at a fraction of AC operating cost.
  • 100% outdoor air systems: Indirect evaporative cooling with heat recovery provides efficient cooling while maintaining ventilation requirements.
  • Hybrid systems: ASHRAE research found that combining desiccant dehumidification with evaporative cooling in Phoenix reduced net electrical power requirements by more than 90% at design conditions (ASHRAE Handbook).

Limitations

  • Humidity ceiling: Above 70°F wet-bulb, direct evaporative cooling provides minimal temperature reduction. Above 50-60% RH, performance drops sharply.
  • Water consumption: DOE data shows annual water use ranging from 2,980 gallons (Santa Fe) to 12,457 gallons (Las Cruces) for a 1,500-square-foot home. Bleed-off valves add 10-50% more (DOE Building America).
  • Maintenance: Pads, pumps, and water distribution systems require regular cleaning and replacement to prevent mineral buildup and biological growth.
  • No dehumidification: Evaporative cooling adds moisture, the opposite of dehumidification. It cannot control humidity — a critical limitation in humid or mixed climates.
