NERC’s 2026-27 Winter Reliability Assessment: What the Grid Outlook Means for Home Heating

The grid’s official winter report card is out. It says there is enough power for a normal winter, fewer regions face elevated risk than last year, and demand keeps climbing. Here is what that means for how you heat your home or building.

Every November, the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) publishes its view of whether the power grid can meet winter demand. This year’s report matters more than usual for heating, because more homes and buildings now depend on electricity for heat, and the same cold snaps that drive up heating demand can also squeeze natural gas supply to power plants.

Who this helps: homeowners with heat pumps or electric heat, gas-heated homes that still need electricity to run the furnace, and building managers planning for cold-weather emergencies.

The headline: better than last year, but demand keeps rising

NERC’s 2026–2027 Winter Reliability Assessment finds adequate resources across North America for normal winter peak conditions. More extreme cold over a wide area could still cause supply shortfalls in some areas. Peak demand across all assessment areas rose 21 GW (2.6%) since last winter, more than the previous year’s 20 GW gain. Resources grew 26 GW of winter on-peak capacity, led by batteries (9.2 GW on-peak) and gas-fired generation (8.7 GW). NERC attributes the demand growth to new data center and industrial loads and to continued electrification across sectors.

According to Latitude Media, just three areas are now rated “elevated” risk during cold snaps, down from seven a year ago. In the U.S., that’s the Carolinas region, where the outlet cites rapid growth in residential electric heating along with new data centers and industrial loads. NRECA’s Electric Co-op Today adds that NERC’s 10-year outlook still expects challenges from plant retirements and data center growth.

Where risk shows up: early winter mornings

the assessment describes the Carolinas area as historically summer-peaking, but now seeing higher winter peaks year over year. Its risk is concentrated in January early-morning hours, when extreme cold raises heating demand, reduces generation availability, and limits imports. That’s the same window when heat pumps work hardest, backup strip heat kicks in, and solar output is near zero.

Our region: PJM and New Jersey

For PJM, the grid operator serving New Jersey, NERC reports about 33% installed reserves under normal conditions, well above the 18.6% target, and no adequacy problems this winter. It does note that reserves are expected to tighten in coming years. In extreme cold with high generator outages, PJM would likely use emergency demand response and could ask large loads to switch to backup generation. Separately, PJM’s winter fuels outlook flags pipeline capacity as a high risk during colder periods, because most pipelines run at or near capacity. It also notes that gas utilities prioritize deliveries to residential heating customers over power plants during very cold periods.

Key takeaway: a gas furnace still needs electricity for its blower, inducer, and controls, so every heating system depends on the grid. The grid is in better shape this winter, but the risk is concentrated in a few extreme cold mornings. Plan for those, not for an average day.

Limitations

NERC’s assessment covers the bulk power system, not local distribution outages from ice or storms, which cause most home outages. The risk categories describe extreme scenarios, not forecasts that outages will happen. NERC also notes that this year’s El Niño pattern makes winter conditions harder to predict.
