HVAC Week in Review: November 11 - 17, 2026

Six stories from the past week that affect equipment supply, what a replacement really costs, this winter’s energy bills and grid, and how technicians use new tools.

This roundup covers HVAC news published November 11 through November 17, 2026. As always, company names appear only because they made the news. HVAC Zone is a multi-brand dealer, and nothing here favors one manufacturer over another.

1. Shipments keep climbing, but furnaces stay flat

ACHR News reports that U.S. shipments of central air conditioners and air-source heat pumps rose 27.3% in September, to 673,286 units. That made eight straight months of year-over-year gains for heat pumps. Year to date, combined shipments are up 7.9%, to 7.06 million, but still below 2024 levels. Gas furnace shipments slipped 0.4% for the month and are down 4.4% for the year. One analyst quoted in the piece said the federal heat pump tax credit ending in 2025 probably pulled some sales forward.

What it means for you: supply is healthy heading into winter. Furnace demand is steady but not growing, and heat pumps still make up a large share of new systems.

2. Homeowners expect lower replacement prices than contractors charge

New homeowner research from ACHR News found that 45% of homeowners expect a replacement system to cost less than $7,500, while contractors report typical prices of $10,000 to $15,000. In the same survey, 58% said only a complete equipment failure would make them consider replacement. 58% use search engines to find a contractor, 54% rely on word of mouth, and 28% may use AI tools. Contractors on the panel recommended diagnostic measurements, such as delivered Btu, to show homeowners how a system is actually performing instead of judging by its age alone.

What it means for you: ask for measurements, not just the equipment’s age, and compare quotes on scope, installation practices, and warranty, not only on price.

3. Winter bills and the power grid

The New York State Department of Public Service expects electric supply costs for an average residential customer to run about $74 a month this winter, up 4.6%. It expects natural gas bills for a typical customer to be about $241 a month, down 3.2%, with actual amounts varying by utility and weather. These figures are for New York, but they point in the same direction as the trends we covered for our region. Nationally, NERC’s 2026–27 Winter Reliability Assessment is due out November 18, according to NERC’s published schedule. We will cover its findings in an upcoming outlook article.

From the field: our winter grid outlook explains what record winter peaks mean for heat pumps and electric heat in New Jersey. If your home relies on electric strip heat as backup, now is a good time to check your aux heat settings.

4. AI troubleshooting: useful, but verify it

ACHR News looked at technicians using AI search tools to troubleshoot refrigeration systems. They’re fast for refreshers on how a component works. But an answer may be wrong or may not apply to the specific system, which can lead to a misdiagnosis or a wrong adjustment. The article advises verifying AI answers against the manufacturer’s manual, or with the manufacturer, a wholesaler, or another technician, before making repairs.

5. Data center cooling keeps scaling up

One manufacturer released a 60 MW (about 17,060 tons) cooling reference design for AI data centers, reports ACHR News. Liquid cooling handles 92% of the load and air cooling the other 8%. Dry coolers carry most of the load for much of the year in suitable climates, with oil-free chillers covering the hottest days. Large projects like this compete for the same engineering talent, controls, and electrical capacity that commercial jobs everywhere depend on.

6. Europe weighs “Made in EU” rules for heat pump subsidies

Refindustry reports that the European Commission’s proposed Industrial Accelerator Act would tie public heat pump support to a minimum of 50% EU added value. Compressors and controls each make up about 25% of a heat pump’s value. The EU has become a net importer of heat pumps since 2020, and a European Parliament committee vote is scheduled for December 1. Rules like this abroad can affect global supply chains and component prices for U.S. equipment too.

Also this week: ACHR News followed up on how the midterm results may affect HVAC policy over the next two years, including refrigerant regulations.

Limitations

Shipment data describes national trends, not local prices or availability. The homeowner survey reflects one publication’s research and panel discussion. The bill projections are for New York utilities and will vary by utility, rate, and weather. The EU proposal has not been adopted and could change.
