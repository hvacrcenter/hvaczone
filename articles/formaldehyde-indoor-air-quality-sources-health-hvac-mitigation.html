Formaldehyde in Homes: Sources, Health Effects, and HVAC Mitigation Strategies

Formaldehyde has been listed as a known human carcinogen since 1981. The EPA reports that levels above 0.1 ppm cause eye and throat irritation, while homes with new pressed wood products can exceed 0.3 ppm. Ventilation, dehumidification, and activated carbon filtration are the primary HVAC mitigation strategies.

Formaldehyde is a colorless, pungent-smelling gas that the National Toxicology Program has classified as a known human carcinogen since 1981. It causes nasopharyngeal cancer, sinonasal cancer, and myeloid leukemia, with sufficient evidence from both human and animal studies (NTP Report on Carcinogens). Yet it remains one of the most common indoor air pollutants — found in pressed wood products, building materials, household products, and combustion sources throughout nearly every home.

Where formaldehyde comes from

The EPA identifies the most significant residential sources as pressed wood products made with urea-formaldehyde (UF) resins: medium density fiberboard (MDF), particleboard, and hardwood plywood paneling. MDF contains a higher resin-to-wood ratio than any other UF pressed wood product and is generally recognized as the highest formaldehyde emitter (EPA). Additional sources include permanent-press drapes and fabrics, glues and adhesives, paints and coatings, unvented gas stoves, kerosene space heaters, and cigarette smoke (EPA).

The EPA reports that elevated levels above 0.1 ppm can cause watery eyes, burning sensations in the eyes and throat, nausea, and difficulty breathing. High concentrations may trigger attacks in people with asthma. Average concentrations in older homes without urea-formaldehyde foam insulation are generally well below 0.1 ppm, but homes with significant amounts of new pressed wood products can exceed 0.3 ppm (EPA). The World Health Organization established an indoor air quality guideline of 0.1 mg/m³ (0.08 ppm) for all 30-minute periods at lifelong exposure, considered protective against both acute and chronic sensory irritation and carcinogenic effects (WHO Guidelines for Indoor Air Quality).

Humidity and temperature amplify emissions.Formaldehyde off-gassing from building materials increases with both temperature and relative humidity. The WHO notes that emissions from new materials can last several months, particularly in conditions with high relative humidity and high indoor temperatures. This means that controlling indoor humidity and temperature through HVAC systems directly reduces formaldehyde emission rates — not just occupant exposure to existing concentrations (WHO Guidelines).

HVAC mitigation strategies

The EPA recommends three primary HVAC-related strategies for reducing formaldehyde in homes:

  • Increase ventilation: Diluting indoor air with outdoor air lowers formaldehyde concentration. The EPA specifically recommends increasing ventilation after bringing new sources of formaldehyde into the home — new furniture, cabinetry, or flooring. Supply ventilation (bringing in outdoor air) is more effective than exhaust ventilation alone for formaldehyde reduction (EPA; ACEEE).
  • Control humidity and temperature: Use air conditioning and dehumidifiers to maintain moderate temperatures and reduce humidity levels. Lower humidity slows the off-gassing rate from pressed wood products (EPA).
  • Activated carbon filtration: Standard HVAC filters (MERV 8 or lower) do not capture gaseous formaldehyde — they only trap particulates. Activated carbon or chemically impregnated media can adsorb formaldehyde, though effectiveness is limited. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study found activated carbon fiber filters achieved only 25-30% removal for formaldehyde (compared to 60-80% for higher-molecular-weight VOCs), but integrating carbon filtration with a 50% reduction in ventilation could lower formaldehyde levels by 12-40% while saving energy (LBNL/DOE).

Source control: the first line of defense

No HVAC strategy can fully compensate for high-emission source materials. The EPA recommends using exterior-grade pressed wood products, which contain phenol-formaldehyde (PF) resins rather than urea-formaldehyde (UF) resins and emit at considerably lower rates. In 2018, EPA finalized regulations under TSCA Title VI setting limits on formaldehyde emissions from composite wood products, with independent third-party certification of compliance (EPA). For existing homes, sealing exposed pressed wood surfaces with low-emission coatings or barriers can reduce off-gassing.

Testing and monitoring

Formaldehyde cannot be detected by standard IAQ monitors that measure CO2, VOCs (as a composite reading), or particulates. Specific formaldehyde testing requires dedicated sensors or laboratory analysis. If occupants experience persistent eye, nose, or throat irritation — particularly in homes with new cabinetry, flooring, or furniture — formaldehyde testing should be considered alongside CO and radon testing as part of a comprehensive IAQ assessment.
