The 2029 HFC Step-Down: What Halving the Refrigerant Supply Means for R-410A Systems

Millions of homes still run R-410A systems. They can be repaired for life, but on January 1, 2029, the national cap on new HFC refrigerant is cut in half. Here is what the rules actually say and how to plan around it.

The American Innovation and Manufacturing (AIM) Act phases down how much hydrofluorocarbon (HFC) refrigerant can be produced and imported in the U.S. R-410A, used in most central air conditioners and heat pumps installed before 2025, is an HFC blend. According to the U.S. EPA’s phasedown FAQ, the cap has been 60% of the historic baseline since 2024. It drops to 30% on January 1, 2029, then to 20% in 2034 and 15% from 2036 on. In EPA’s estimates, the consumption cap falls from about 181.5 to 90.8 million metric tons of exchange value equivalent.

Who this helps: homeowners with an R-410A system that is 8 to 15 years old, landlords planning capital budgets, and anyone weighing a major repair.

What the rules do and don’t do

The cap limits supply, not use. EPA’s Technology Transitions fact sheet is explicit: the rule “does not restrict the continued use of any existing products or RACHP systems,” and a system “may be serviced and repaired throughout its useful life; this includes replacing components, as needed.” Repair parts for existing equipment can still be made and sold. New residential split systems, by contrast, have been limited to refrigerants with a global warming potential of 700 or less, which is why new equipment uses A2L refrigerants.

In short, no one is required to replace a working R-410A system, in 2029 or after. What changes is how much new R-410A can come into the market.

Reclaimed refrigerant moves to the center

Recovered refrigerant cleaned to a purity standard by a certified reclaimer is called reclaimed refrigerant. Under EPA’s 2024 Emissions Reduction and Reclamation rule and its reclamation fact sheet, since January 1, 2026, refrigerant sold as “reclaimed” may contain no more than 15% virgin HFC by weight, and containers must be labeled. From January 1, 2029, servicing supermarket systems, refrigerated transport, and commercial ice makers must use reclaimed HFCs. Residential air conditioning is not on that list, but it will draw from the same, smaller pool of refrigerant.

For now, supply looks healthy. ACHR News reported in August that nearly 80% of distributors surveyed by HARDI had R-410A inventories at or above target. HARDI described a “significant glut” of virgin R-410A. AHRI cautioned that prices may rise as supplies are reduced. HARDI’s government affairs lead said that after 2029 service “will rely on the remaining glut and reclaimed refrigerant,” and that “every pound vented today is a pound that cannot be used in the future.”

Key takeaway: 2029 is a supply date, not a ban. Any R-410A system can still be repaired after 2029. The question is cost: a system that needs regular top-offs today will likely cost more to keep running later. Fixing the leak is almost always better than refilling it.

Repair or replace: a planning checklist

SituationWhat to consider
System under 10 years, no leaksKeep it maintained; no reason to replace on refrigerant grounds alone
Needs refrigerant every seasonHave the leak located and repaired; repeated top-offs only get more expensive
Major failure on an older systemCompare the repair cost plus refrigerant against a replacement quote
Planning a replacement in 2–4 yearsBudget ahead and replace on your schedule, not during a failure
Old system being removedMake sure the refrigerant is recovered by a certified technician

What to watch through 2028

EPA’s reclamation fact sheet says the rule requires reclamation reporting so the agency can evaluate the supply of reclaimed refrigerant ahead of the 2029 servicing requirement. Recovery rates matter: the more refrigerant technicians recover and send for reclamation now, the larger the service supply will be later. State rules can add requirements too; ACHR News lists New Jersey among states with draft refrigerant rules or active campaigns.

Limitations

Nobody can predict exact refrigerant prices. They depend on demand, imports, inventory, and how much refrigerant gets reclaimed. The EPA documents cited summarize the rules and should not be relied on alone for compliance; the regulations themselves are in 40 CFR Part 84.
