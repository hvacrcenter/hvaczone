Vent-Free Gas and Kerosene Space Heaters: What They Put Into Your Indoor Air, and What ASHRAE Now Recommends

Vent-free gas heaters, gas logs, and portable kerosene or propane heaters are close to 100% efficient for one reason: everything they burn stays in the room. That includes nitrogen dioxide, carbon monoxide, and water vapor. Here is what the measurements and the latest ASHRAE position say.

Every winter, some homes use vent-free gas heaters, gas logs, and portable kerosene or propane heaters to warm a cold room or ride out a power outage. They give off a lot of heat for little money. The catch is where the exhaust goes: with no chimney or flue, the combustion products stay in the room you’re breathing in.

Who this helps: households using or considering unvented heaters, landlords, people with asthma or other lung conditions, and anyone planning backup heat for outages.

What unvented heaters release

According to the EPA, the main pollutants from combustion appliances are carbon monoxide, nitrogen dioxide, and particles. The EPA adds that in homes with kerosene heaters or unvented gas space heaters, indoor NO2 levels often exceed outdoor levels. It says venting NO2 sources outdoors is the most effective way to reduce exposure. The New York State Department of Health warns that overusing a heater, or using one that’s too big for the room, can raise CO or NO2 levels and cause headaches, flu-like symptoms, and eye, nose, and throat irritation.

What measurements show

In June 2026, ASHRAE revised its position document on unvented combustion devices. It cites a study of 30 Illinois homes with unvented gas heaters. In 80% of the homes, NO2 rose above EPA, WHO, and Health Canada 1-hour guideline levels within just 3 to 4 days of sampling. In 20% of the homes, carbon monoxide went over the 9 ppm 8-hour reference level, mostly when the heaters ran for long stretches. For kerosene, a peer-reviewed review of household kerosene use reports that personal NO2 exposure in homes using these heaters was five to six times higher than in homes without them.

Built-in safeguards, and their limits

The ASHRAE position document explains that gas-fired unvented room heaters must be listed to ANSI Z21.11.2. That standard requires an oxygen depletion sensor (ODS), which shuts off the burner when room oxygen drops to 18%. It also caps heater size at 40,000 Btu/h, or 10,000 Btu/h in bedrooms and 6,000 Btu/h in bathrooms. But an ODS reacts to low oxygen, not to NO2 or moisture, so it doesn’t prevent the exposures measured in the study above.

ASHRAE’s bottom line: vented devices cause less exposure than unvented ones. ASHRAE says unvented combustion devices should never be the primary or only source of heat, and that people who want to reduce health risk should not use them. It also says every home with an unvented device should have a CO alarm.

Why it matters for carbon monoxide

The Consumer Product Safety Commission estimated 274 consumer-product CO deaths in 2022. Among gas-fueled products, 75 of 112 deaths (67%) involved heating systems or heaters, including furnaces, portable heaters, and room or space heaters. EPA advice is direct: don’t sleep in any room with an unvented gas or kerosene space heater.

Practical checklist

  • Install CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, and test them monthly.
  • Never use an unvented heater as your main heat, in a bedroom, or while sleeping.
  • Crack a window while one is running, and never use an oven, grill, or generator for heat indoors.
  • If someone in the home has asthma, choose vented or electric options.
  • After air-sealing work, have ventilation reassessed if any unvented device remains, as ASHRAE recommends.
  • If a room needs a space heater every winter, fix the cause: ductwork, insulation, or a dedicated zone.

Limitations

Results depend on heater size, how long it runs, room volume, and air leakage. The cited studies may not match your home. Local codes differ on whether vent-free appliances are allowed and where, so check with your local code official before installing one.
