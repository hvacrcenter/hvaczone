Mini-Split Flare Joints: Flaring Tools, Torque Wrenches, and Press Fittings for Leak-Free A2L Installs

On a mini-split, the flare nuts at the indoor head are often the weakest link in the whole system. With new equipment moving to mildly flammable A2L refrigerants, the tools and steps used to make those joints matter more than ever.

Ductless mini-splits and many heat pumps arrive with copper line sets that connect to the indoor and outdoor units with field-made flare joints. A flare is a metal-to-metal seal: a 45° cone on the end of the tube pressed against a matching fitting by a flare nut. No tape, no sealant. When it’s made and tightened correctly it holds for years. When it isn’t, it becomes a slow leak that shows up months later as weak heating, a low charge, and a service call.

Who this helps: homeowners getting a ductless or heat pump quote, building owners writing install specs, and technicians tooling up for A2L equipment.

Why the tools matter now

New residential equipment is moving to A2L refrigerants, which are mildly flammable. UL Solutions explains that the current product safety standard, UL 60335-2-40, adds “enhanced requirements” for tubing, joints and fittings to reduce potential leak sources by minimizing the number of joints. For field-made indoor joints, the UL 60335-2-40 requirements, as summarized in an Intertek test checklist, say that when mechanical connectors are reused indoors their sealing parts must be renewed, and when flared joints are reused indoors “the flare part shall be re-fabricated.” In plain terms: if an indoor flare on an A2L system is taken apart, a new flare gets made before it goes back together.

New York City’s housing agency has put this into a field spec. The NYC HPD Technical Requirements: Refrigerant Charging and Leak Prevention covers heat pump projects it funds. It requires technicians to sign that they have read it and to keep EPA 608 certification on site. It notes that A2L refrigerants make leak prevention “especially important.”

Flaring tools

The HPD requirements recommend making new flares on site rather than trusting factory pre-flares, which “vary in quality and may be damaged in transit.” They call for a flaring tool with three features:

  • A depth gauge or stop so the tube sits at the right height.
  • An eccentric cone that rolls the copper rather than just pushing it.
  • A clutch that releases when the flare is complete.

Flares should be checked with a flare sizing gauge. HPD also notes that battery-powered flaring tools, used and maintained properly, produce consistent flares. Before assembly the flare should be symmetrical, shiny, and scratch-free, with a thin coat of refrigeration oil or approved lubricant on the face.

Torque wrenches

“Good and tight” by feel isn’t a spec. Too little torque won’t seal, and too much can crack or split the flare. HPD calls for a torque wrench with a digital gauge, tightened to the manufacturer’s value. Those values typically run from about 13 ft-lb for 1/4-inch tubing to about 56 ft-lb for 5/8-inch. It also says to use only the flare nuts supplied with the equipment, because the torque specs are written for those nuts. If a flare leaks later, the rule is clear: don’t tighten it more. Cut it out and make a new one.

Flare, braze, or press?

Joint typeStrengthsWatch-outs
FlareNo flame; standard at mini-split unit connectionsDepends on tool quality, flare gauge check, and correct torque; re-make if reused indoors on A2L

Key takeaway: on mini-split and A2L installs, the leak-tight joint is the result of the right tool used the right way: a clutch-type eccentric flaring tool, a flare gauge, the OEM nut, and a torque wrench set to the manual’s value. Then prove it with a nitrogen pressure test and a vacuum decay test.

Proving the joints

Making the joint is only half the job. HPD asks for a standing nitrogen test at 550 psig or higher, as specified by the manufacturer. The test runs at least 1 hour for line sets under 200 feet and 24 hours for longer systems, read on a digital gauge with 0.1 psi resolution. After evacuation, the system must stay below 200 microns at least 15 minutes after being isolated. Every flare also gets an approved bubble solution, not dish soap. Our nitrogen testing guide covers those steps in more detail.

Questions to ask your installer

  • Will you make new flares on site and torque them with a torque wrench?
  • Will any joints be hidden in walls, and if so, is there an access panel?
  • Will I get the nitrogen pressure test and vacuum test results in writing?

Limitations

The HPD document applies to NYC HPD-funded projects, not to every job. Your local code and the equipment’s installation manual come first. Torque values vary by manufacturer and tube size, so always follow the specific unit’s literature.
