HVAC Week in Review: July 20–26, 2026

A refrigerant rule under legal fire, an efficiency rulemaking open for comment, a European electrification target, and new cross-border tariffs — all moving this week, and each one reaches into how buildings are heated, cooled, and supplied.

The biggest HVAC stories this week came from courthouses, federal agencies, and a European Commission plan — not from product launches. A refrigerant rule took effect the same week states sued to block it. The Department of Energy has an open comment window on how it sets efficiency standards. The EU set a heat pump target nearly double today's rate. And new tariffs on Canadian goods added another layer of supply-chain pressure. Here's what's active this week in heating, cooling, and IAQ.

Refrigerant & regulation

18 states sue EPA over HFC refrigerant rule. On July 21, a coalition of 18 states plus the District of Columbia and New York City filed a legal challenge in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit against an EPA rule that relaxed global-warming-potential (GWP) limits for commercial refrigeration systems. California Attorney General Rob Bonta co-led the coalition with Massachusetts and Washington, arguing that the rule — which raised GWP thresholds for supermarket and remote-condensing systems from 150/300 to 1,400 and delayed stricter compliance to 2032 — violates the American Innovation and Manufacturing (AIM) Act by ignoring climate impacts (LA Times). The rule took effect July 27. For HVACR contractors, the litigation adds uncertainty to an already-shifting refrigerant landscape — the same AIM Act framework that governs the R-410A phase-down we covered in our A2L transition article. Commercial refrigeration is a different equipment class, but the legal challenge signals that states may push back if they believe federal rules weaken refrigerant climate goals.

DOE Process Rule NOPR — comments due August 6. The Department of Energy issued a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NOPR) proposing to update its "Process Rule" — the procedural framework at 10 CFR 430 Appendix A that guides how DOE sets energy-efficiency standards for consumer products and commercial equipment (DOE Process Rule). Key proposals include making the Process Rule binding on DOE, adding a definition of "significant energy savings," reinstating a "walk-up" comparative analysis approach, and reverting to 2020-era text on economic thresholds and "clear and convincing evidence" standards. DOE held a public webinar on July 15 and is accepting comments through August 6, 2026 (docket EERE-2025-BT-STD-0001). For the HVAC industry, this matters because the Process Rule determines how aggressively — and by what methodology — future efficiency standards for air conditioners, furnaces, and heat pumps are set. A more restrictive process could slow or narrow future standard increases.

Electrification & trade

EU Electrification Action Plan targets 4M heat pumps per year by 2030. On July 17, the European Commission presented its Electrification Action Plan, calling for annual heat pump installations to rise from 2.4 million in 2025 to 4 million by 2030 (European Commission). The plan sets price-ratio targets — electricity below 2.5x gas for households and below 2x for industry — and says it will explore a "clean heat market mechanism" to incentivize rising heat pump sales, a dedicated Cooling and Heating Action Plan, and integration of buildings into demand-response programs (EC Factsheet; EU BUILD UP). While this is European policy, the scale matters globally: a near-doubling of heat pump installations signals manufacturing capacity shifts, refrigerant demand changes, and technology cost curves that affect the U.S. market. It also reinforces the electrification trend we explored in our cold-climate heat pump and heat pump water heater articles.

50% tariff on many Canadian goods. The Trump administration announced new 50% tariffs on many Canadian imports, citing what it calls discriminatory Canadian trade practices against U.S. products, as reported by Contracting Business (Contracting Business). Key sectors including energy and vehicles are exempt, but the tariffs affect chemicals, plastics, cement, and a range of industrial goods — materials that feed into HVAC equipment manufacturing, ductwork, insulation, and mechanical-system components. The move adds another cost variable on top of the copper tariffs and supply-chain pressures we examined in our supply-chain outlook article. For contractors, the concern is whether higher input costs eventually translate into equipment price increases or longer lead times.

What contractors and homeowners should watch next. The D.C. Circuit case could take months to resolve — in the meantime, the EPA rule stands. The DOE Process Rule comment window closes August 6, so industry input is still possible. The EU plan is aspirational until member states adopt implementing legislation. And tariff impacts typically lag the announcement by weeks to months as existing inventory clears. None of these changes what a good install looks like today — but together they signal a market where refrigerant availability, equipment efficiency rules, and material costs are all in motion.

The bottom line

This week's HVAC headlines were written by lawyers, regulators, and trade negotiators — not engineers. A refrigerant rule took effect under lawsuit. An efficiency rulemaking is open for public comment. A European plan set an ambitious heat pump target. And new tariffs added cost pressure to an already strained supply chain. None changes what a quality installation requires today — a proper load calculation, a compatible refrigerant, sealed ducts, a filter the blower can handle — but each one will shape which equipment is available, how it's regulated, and what it costs over the coming years.
