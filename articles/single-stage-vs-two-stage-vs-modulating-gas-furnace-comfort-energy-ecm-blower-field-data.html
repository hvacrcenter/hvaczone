Single-Stage vs. Two-Stage vs. Modulating Gas Furnaces: What the Test and Field Data Actually Show

Furnace quotes usually come in three tiers: single-stage, two-stage, and modulating. The comfort difference is real. The energy story is more complicated, and the biggest factor is often the blower and the ductwork, not the burner.

A single-stage furnace has one firing rate: full fire or off. A two-stage furnace adds a low-fire setting, generally about 65% of full output according to Energy Trust of Oregon. A modulating furnace can vary its firing rate across a range. Contractors (us included) often describe the upgrade in terms of comfort and savings. The comfort part holds up well. The savings part depends on the blower and the ducts much more than on the burner.

Who this helps: homeowners comparing furnace quotes, and technicians explaining options to customers.

What staging does well: comfort

Most heating hours happen in mild weather, when a furnace sized for the coldest day is oversized for the load. A single-stage unit short-cycles: a burst of hot air, then off. Energy Trust of Oregon, citing a Wisconsin field study, reports that two-stage furnaces run on low fire about 80% of the time, so the blower runs about 40% more hours per year, at lower speed. Longer, gentler cycles mean smaller temperature swings, better air mixing between rooms, and quieter operation. They also mean more hours of air passing through the filter.

What staging does not do: save much gas by itself

An analysis by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory compared two-stage and single-stage furnaces at the same AFUE under two test methods. The DOE procedure showed about 3% lower total site energy for two-stage. The 2006 ASHRAE procedure, which the authors considered more accurate, showed almost no difference. Their conclusion: "two-stage technology by itself does not save energy," but two-stage paired with an efficient variable-speed (ECM/BPM) blower does save electricity.

The small gas penalty with ECM motors happens because an efficient motor gives off less waste heat into the airstream, so the burner makes up the difference. For a gas bill, AFUE matters far more than staging. ENERGY STAR says certified gas furnaces in northern states are up to 18% more efficient than baseline models.

The catch: duct static pressure

The Energy Center of Wisconsin field study measured 31 newer furnaces. At 1,000 cfm, an ECM air handler drew about 175 watts less than a PSC unit, and it saved roughly 400 kWh per year in heating mode in a typical Wisconsin home. Far more was saved with continuous fan operation. But field static pressure averaged 0.5 inches of water column, against 0.20 to 0.23 in. in the federal test. The median ECM furnace used 82% more electricity per therm than its rating, because the motor ramps up to push air through restrictive ducts and filters. A Gas Technology Institute test plan for Building America likewise expected blower power to rise with static pressure and to vary significantly by furnace type in tight duct systems.

Field note: a variable-speed blower on a duct system running at 0.8 to 1.0 in. w.c. will move the air, but it pays for it in watts and noise. Measure total external static pressure before choosing equipment, and fix the return side first if it's choking the system.

Limitations

The lab analysis and the Wisconsin field study are older and predate current motor efficiency rules. Today's blowers and test procedures differ, and newer furnaces may perform better. Results depend on climate, sizing, duct design, filter choice, and thermostat setup. Federal furnace efficiency rules are also changing. See the DOE rulemaking page and our 95% AFUE rule explainer.
