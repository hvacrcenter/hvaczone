Wildfire Smoke and Your HVAC: How Filtration Responds to PM2.5 Events

When wildfire smoke pushes outdoor PM2.5 past 200 µg/m³, your HVAC filter becomes the first line of defense. EPA data shows indoor PM2.5 typically reaches 55-60% of outdoor levels during smoke events. ASHRAE recommends MERV 13 or higher — but new research reveals the filter media type matters as much as the rating. Here's what the evidence says.

Wildfire smoke used to be a Western U.S. problem. In 2023, smoke from Canadian wildfires pushed PM2.5 levels in Boston-area homes to 9.9 µg/m³ during smoke days — nearly triple the 3.5 µg/m³ measured on non-smoke days (Harvard T.H. Chan / NIH). A 2026 study of Los Angeles wildfire survivors found that indoor air quality statistically explained most of the association between outdoor PM2.5 and health symptoms (Environmental International). Your HVAC filter is the barrier between those two numbers.

What wildfire smoke does to indoor air

Wildfire smoke is dominated by PM2.5 — particles smaller than 2.5 micrometers that penetrate deep into the lungs. EPA's 2026 guide for public health officials reports that indoor PM2.5 concentrations during wildfire smoke events typically run 55-60% of outdoor levels when doors and windows are closed and no portable air cleaner is running. The range is wide: 30% in tight, newer homes with central AC to 100% in older, leaky homes (EPA).

During the 2020 Washington wildfire episode, outdoor PM2.5 exceeded 200 µg/m³ for multiple days. A University of Washington sensor network found that homes with HEPA filtration had a median indoor/outdoor ratio of 0.43, with 58% PM2.5 reduction — compared to 0.82 I/O ratio and only 20% reduction in homes without filtration (Atmospheric Environment).

What ASHRAE recommends

ASHRAE's planning framework for protecting building occupants from wildfire smoke (Guideline 44P committee) recommends MERV 13 or higher filters in HVAC recirculation systems during smoke events. If the system cannot accommodate MERV 13, use the highest MERV-rated filter possible — MERV 11 to 13 and higher can remove smoke particles (ASHRAE).

ASHRAE's specific operational guidance:

  • Recirculation filters:Install MERV 13+ on the recirculation side — this is where the filtration work happens
  • Outdoor-air intakes:Add a minimum MERV 13 filter on outdoor-air intakes to capture a large fraction of incoming PM2.5
  • Filter replacement:Change when pressure drop reaches 2x the initial pressure drop; during heavy smoke, filters may need changing as frequently as daily
  • System checks:Verify the blower can handle the higher pressure drop — an HVAC or TAB technician can evaluate this before wildfire season
  • Damper control:Reduce outdoor-air ventilation during smoke events but don't go negative — negative pressure pulls smoke through cracks and gaps
  • Fan operation:Run the system fan continuously to maximize filtration cycles

MERV rating vs. real-world smoke performance

A 2025 study of 24 campus buildings at the University of Alberta found that MERV 13 filters produced an indoor/outdoor PM2.5 ratio of 0.12 ± 0.07 during wildfire events — meaning only 12% of outdoor smoke penetrated indoors. MERV 8 filters allowed 28% ± 14% infiltration. However, 71% of buildings still exceeded the Canadian Ambient Air Quality Standards daily limit of 27 µg/m³ despite having mechanical ventilation with filters (ACS ES&T Air).

The media-type problem.A 2025 study from Semantic Scholar tested 17 commercially available HVAC filter media against pine needle smoke as a wildfire proxy. Charged polymer (electrostatic) media showed a rapid decline in smoke removal efficiency within minutes — the electrostatic charge that boosts the MERV rating gets neutralized by smoke particles. Mechanical (fiberglass) media held efficiency but increased airflow resistance. The authors concluded that current MERV testing — based on inorganic particles — may not predict smoke filtration performance, and called for smoke-specific test standards (Semantic Scholar).

This is a critical finding for homeowners: a MERV 13 electrostatic filter may perform no better than a MERV 8 mechanical filter after the first few minutes of smoke exposure. If you're upgrading for wildfire season, ask for mechanical (fiberglass) media rather than charged synthetic — and verify the system can handle the higher pressure drop.

Practical checklist before wildfire smoke hits

  • Upgrade to MERV 13 mechanical mediaon your recirculation filter — verify the blower can handle the pressure drop with your HVAC technician
  • Stock extra filters— ASHRAE warns filters may need daily changing during heavy smoke; supplies sell out during events
  • Install a pressure gaugeon at least one air handler to monitor filter loading; replace at 2x initial pressure drop
  • Add a MERV 13 filter on outdoor-air intakeif your system has one — this captures incoming PM2.5 before it enters the ductwork
  • Seal filter edges— air leakage around filters greatly reduces effectiveness; check filter access doors and gaskets
  • Prepare to run the fan continuously— this maximizes filtration cycles even when heating/cooling isn't active
  • Have a portable HEPA cleaneras backup for the room where you'll shelter — the HVAC system alone may not keep up during extreme events
  • Know your system's economizer— disable free-cooling economizer operation during smoke events to avoid pulling in unfiltered outdoor air

The bottom line

Wildfire smoke is no longer a regional issue. The 2023 Canadian wildfires degraded air quality from Boston to Chicago, and the 2025 Los Angeles fires showed that indoor air quality — not outdoor concentration — is what drives health symptoms. Your HVAC system can be part of the solution or part of the problem. A MERV 13 mechanical filter, properly seated and monitored, can cut indoor smoke exposure by 80-90%. But only if the system can handle it, and only if you change the filter before it loads.
