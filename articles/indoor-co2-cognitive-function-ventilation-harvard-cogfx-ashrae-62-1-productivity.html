Indoor CO2 and Cognitive Function: How Ventilation Rates Affect Productivity and Health

Harvard's COGfx studies found cognitive scores dropped 15% at 950 ppm CO2 and 50% at 1,400 ppm — levels common in offices and classrooms. A global study of 302 workers across six countries found every 500 ppm increase slows response times 1.4-1.8%. Here's what the science says about ventilation, CO2, and why ASHRAE 62.1's minimum rates may not be enough.

Carbon dioxide has long been used as a proxy for ventilation rate — the higher the indoor CO2, the less outdoor air is being delivered per person. But a growing body of research from Harvard, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, and others suggests CO2 may be more than a proxy: it may be a direct pollutant that impairs cognitive function at concentrations commonly found in offices, classrooms, and homes. This has profound implications for how HVAC systems are designed, sized, and operated.

The Harvard COGfx findings

The landmark COGfx Study 1, published in Environmental Health Perspectives by Joseph Allen and colleagues at Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, put 24 knowledge workers through six full workdays in a controlled office environment. On "Green+" days with doubled outdoor air ventilation (40 cfm/person, corresponding to ~550 ppm CO2), cognitive scores were 101% higher than on "Conventional" days with standard ventilation (20 cfm/person) and elevated VOCs. The researchers also independently manipulated CO2 while holding ventilation constant: at approximately 950 ppm — a level common in occupied indoor spaces — cognitive performance dropped 15%. At 1,400 ppm, scores fell 50% ([Allen et al., EHP 2016](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120379-1584-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b)).

Outdoor CO2 is currently around 400 ppm. Indoors, levels routinely run 800-1,200 ppm, and it is not uncommon to see 1,500 to 3,000 ppm in densely occupied or under-ventilated spaces ([Harvard COGfx](https://healthybuildings.hsph.harvard.edu/research/indoor-air-quality/cogfx/)). The study found that the domains most affected — crisis response, strategy, and information usage — are precisely those most tied to workplace productivity.

Global evidence: 302 workers, six countries

The COGfx Study 3 expanded the research globally, enrolling 302 office workers across China, India, Mexico, Thailand, the UK, and the US. Using real-time environmental sensors and ecological momentary assessment, the team found that for every 10 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5, response times slowed 0.8-0.9% and throughput dropped 0.8-1.7%. For every 500 ppm increase in CO2, response times slowed 1.4-1.8% and throughput dropped 2.1-2.4%. Critically, the researchers found no lower threshold at which the effects of low ventilation disappeared — meaning even buildings meeting current minimum standards may be leaving cognitive performance on the table ([Cedeño Laurent et al., Environmental Research Letters, 2021](https://healthybuildings.hsph.harvard.edu/impacts-of-indoor-air-quality-on-cognitive-function/); [Harvard SPH News](https://hsph.harvard.edu/news/office-air-quality-may-affect-employees-cognition-productivity/)).

As the Harvard team noted: "The increased productivity of an employee is over 150x greater than the resulting energy costs" ([Harvard COGfx](https://healthybuildings.hsph.harvard.edu/research/indoor-air-quality/cogfx/)).

ASHRAE 62.1: what the standard actually says

A common misconception is that ASHRAE Standard 62.1 sets a CO2 limit of 1,000 ppm. It does not. The standard has not contained an indoor CO2 limit for nearly 30 years — the limit was removed because it caused confusion and CO2 alone is not a reliable indicator of overall IAQ. The 1,000 ppm figure that many cite comes from the relationship that a ventilation rate of 7.5 L/s (15 cfm) per person in an office space produces a steady-state CO2 concentration of roughly 1,000 ppm above an outdoor baseline of ~400 ppm. But other space types have different ventilation requirements, and the corresponding CO2 levels range from ~700 ppm to 5,000 ppm depending on occupancy density ([ASHRAE Position Document on Indoor CO2](https://www.ashrae.org/file%20library/about/position%20documents/pd-on-indoor-carbon-dioxide-english.pdf); [NIST IA2020](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=929997)).

ASHRAE 62.1 uses the Ventilation Rate Procedure, which calculates breathing-zone outdoor airflow as: Vbz = (Rp × Pz) + (Ra × Az), where Rp is the per-person rate, Pz is zone population, Ra is the per-area rate, and Az is zone floor area. A 2023 addendum added "Maximum CO2 Above Ambient" differentials for use as setpoints in demand-controlled ventilation (DCV) — but these are explicitly for DCV control, not as IAQ indicators ([ASHRAE Addendum to 62.1-2022](https://www.ashrae.org/file%20library/technical%20resources/standards%20and%20guidelines/standards%20addenda/62_1_2022_ab_20231031.pdf)).

DOE ventilation benchmarks

The DOE's September 2025 Ventilation Assessment and Action Guide translates ASHRAE 62.1-2022 requirements into practical CO2 benchmarks: offices should maintain >17 cfm/person with peak CO2 ≤965 ppm; schools >15 cfm/person with ≤1,010 ppm (lower grades) or ≤1,210 ppm (upper grades); hospitals >2 air changes per hour with ≤580 ppm. The guide notes that high CO2 is a good indicator of ventilation problems and that ASHRAE 62.1 requires testing and balancing every five years ([DOE Ventilation Guide, Sept 2025](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2025-09/ventilation-assessment-and-action-guide_september-2025.pdf)).

HVAC design implications

  • Increase ventilation beyond minimums: Harvard found benefits at 40 cfm/person — double the ASHRAE 62.1 office minimum of ~17 cfm/person — with no lower threshold for improvement.
  • Use CO2-based DCV: Demand-controlled ventilation modulates outdoor air based on real-time CO2, delivering full ventilation during occupancy while saving energy when spaces are lightly occupied. ASHRAE 62.1's 2023 addendum provides DCV setpoint guidance.
  • Pair with enhanced filtration: The global study found PM2.5 independently impairs cognition. MERV 13+ filtration captures most fine particulate and complements higher ventilation rates.
  • Monitor, don't assume: Low-cost CO2 loggers reveal under-ventilation that design calculations miss. DOE recommends monitoring as a diagnostic tool.
  • Consider energy recovery: Doubling ventilation increases conditioning load. ERVs and DOAS units recover energy from exhaust air, making higher ventilation rates economically viable — the $30/person/year energy cost cited by Harvard already accounts for standard HVAC, and energy recovery can reduce that further.
  • Test and balance regularly: ASHRAE 62.1 requires TAB every 5 years. Dampers, fans, and controls drift over time, silently reducing delivered ventilation.
