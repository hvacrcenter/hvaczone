Window Heat Pumps for Apartments: What Field Data Shows About Saddle-Style Room Heat Pumps

A heat pump that hangs over a windowsill and plugs into a regular outlet sounds too simple. Public housing pilots and utility field studies now have real data on how these units perform in cold weather, and where they fall short.

Many older apartment buildings run on central steam or electric baseboard heat, with window air conditioners in summer. Converting them to conventional heat pumps can mean new refrigerant lines, electrical upgrades, and weeks of work inside occupied units. Packaged window heat pumps, also called saddle-style or room heat pumps, take a different approach. The whole system sits in one unit that straddles a sash windowsill, with the compressor outside and the indoor section inside, and it plugs into a standard 120-volt outlet.

Who this helps: apartment owners and landlords, condo and co-op boards, tenants with steam or baseboard heat, and homeowners looking to heat or cool a single room or addition.

The New York pilot: efficiency and comfort

The best-documented test is New York’s Clean Heat for All pilot at NYCHA’s Woodside Houses in Queens, where 72 units from two manufacturers went into 24 apartments. According to NYSERDA, the window heat pumps cut heating energy intensity by more than 75% compared with the previous two-pipe steam system. The independent measurement and verification report by Taitem Engineering (NYSERDA Report 25-21) found:

  • Apartments averaged about 74.5°F in winter. 89% of surveyed residents said the temperature was “just right” and 11% said too warm; 81% were satisfied.
  • Rated heating COP was above 4 at 47°F and about 2 at 5°F, with both models able to run down to −13°F.
  • Peak demand at building risers rose about 50% on average, but no breakers tripped and conductors stayed within capacity.
  • NYCHA’s preliminary cost savings estimate was about 50%.

Based on the pilot, NYCHA announced in April 2026 a five-year plan to install heat pumps in 20,000 apartments.

Northwest homes: do-it-yourself installs in three climate zones

A Washington State University and NEEA field study put 26 units in homes across Washington, Oregon, Idaho, and Montana, nearly all installed by the residents themselves. Most active heating ran at reduced capacity below 500 watts, maximum power was drawn less than 2% of the time, and peak momentary current stayed under 13 amps. 25 of 26 participants reported better winter comfort. Uptime was more than 99%, though four units needed trained service, and condensate pump noise and filter clogging were the most common complaints. Of 11 homes with full billing histories, 8 used fewer kWh, but only 5 saw lower bills, partly because electricity rates rose.

Boston: controls matter as much as hardware

A Boston Housing Authority pilot documented by New Ecology installed one unit per apartment in 100 apartments while keeping the existing electric baseboards. At first, fewer than 10% of apartments heated mainly with the heat pump, because the baseboard thermostats competed with it. After baseboard setpoint limits were lowered, heat pump use rose above 40% by early March. Canary Media reported the Boston retrofit cost about $5,450 per apartment.

Key takeaway: window heat pumps can heat efficiently in real winters and usually avoid electrical service upgrades. Savings depend on the existing heat being set back or locked out so it doesn’t compete with the heat pump.

Fit checklist

FactorWhat field studies found
Window typeSash windows only; the Northwest study recommends at least 26.5 inches of width, plus room inside and out
Room sizeAbout 9,000 Btu/h; design guidance near 750 sq ft; may be oversized for small bedrooms
WeightMore than 120 lb; secure bracing matters on thin walls or sills
ElectricalStandard 120V outlet; can share a 15A circuit only if other loads are small
Existing heatSet baseboards or steam 5–7°F below the heat pump, or limit them with controls
MaintenanceClean filters, including condensate filters, more often than the manual minimum
CodesStill unsettled: whether building departments treat units as appliances or permanent equipment, and how window and egress rules apply

Limitations

Field samples are small, and long-term durability and service networks are still unproven. The Taitem report notes the first pilot winter was mild and that energy use varied tenfold between apartments. These units heat individual rooms, not whole houses. They don’t replace a properly sized central or ductless system in a typical single-family home.
