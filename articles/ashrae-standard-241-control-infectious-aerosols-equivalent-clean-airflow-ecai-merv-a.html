ASHRAE Standard 241 Explained: Equivalent Clean Airflow (ECAi), MERV-A Filtration, and Infectious Aerosol Control

For nearly a century, non-healthcare ventilation standards focused on odor and chemical dilution rather than airborne pathogen control. ASHRAE Standard 241 changes that by defining minimum Equivalent Clean Airflow (ECAi) rates of 10 to 45 L/s (20 to 90 CFM) per person during Infection Risk Management Mode — allowing buildings to combine outdoor air, MERV-A 11+ filtration, and verified air cleaning without the massive energy penalty of 100% outdoor air.

Traditional indoor air quality standards — ANSI/ASHRAE Standard 62.1 for commercial buildings and Standard 62.2 for residential buildings — were written to control perceived indoor air quality (body odor) and common chemical and particulate contaminants. As ASHRAE notes in its official history of the standard, airborne infection risk management had been absent from non-healthcare IAQ standards since the 1930s ([ASHRAE Standard 241 Fact Sheet](https://www.ashrae.org/file%20library/about/government%20affairs/advocacy%20toolkit/virtual%20packet/standard-241-fact-sheet.pdf)). Published in 2023 and updated through 2024–2025 addenda, ASHRAE Standard 241, Control of Infectious Aerosols establishes the first consensus engineering framework for reducing long-range airborne transmission of respiratory pathogens such as SARS-CoV-2 and influenza in homes, offices, schools, and commercial buildings ([ASHRAE News](https://www.ashrae.org/about/news/2023/ashrae-publishes-standard-241-control-of-infectious-aerosols)).

Equivalent Clean Airflow (ECAi): why it replaces raw outdoor air

Bringing in 30 to 90 CFM per person of unconditioned outdoor air during a winter cold snap or summer heat wave would overwhelm most HVAC heating and cooling coils and multiply utility bills. Standard 241 solves this by introducing Equivalent Clean Airflow for Infection Risk Mitigation (ECAi) — defined as the theoretical flow rate of pathogen-free air that, if distributed uniformly within the breathing zone, has the same effect on infectious aerosol concentration as the sum of actual outdoor airflow, filtered recirculated air, and pathogen inactivation ([Jones et al., Building and Environment 2025](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/407074832/1-s2.0-S036013232500798X-main.pdf); [ASHRAE Position Document on Filtration and Air Cleaning](https://www.ashrae.org/File%20Library/About/Position%20Documents/pd-on-filtration-and-air-cleaning-english.pdf)).

Under Standard 241, a building must first meet its baseline outdoor air requirement under ASHRAE 62.1, 62.2, or 170. Then, during Infection Risk Management Mode (IRMM) — activated during periods of elevated respiratory disease transmission — the HVAC system uses upgraded mechanical filtration, germicidal ultraviolet (GUV/UVGI) disinfection, or certified in-room air cleaners to supply the remaining pathogen-free airflow needed to reach the space's \( \text{ECAi} \) target ([CDC/NIOSH Ventilation FAQ](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ventilation/faq/index.html); [ELI/ASHRAE SSPC 241 Presentation](https://www.eli.org/sites/default/files/files-pdf/Kazukiyo%20Kumagai%20PPT_0.pdf)).

How the ECAi numbers were modeled

A 2025 peer-reviewed paper in Building and Environment by the ASHRAE Standard 241 risk modeling group details the Monte Carlo Wells–Riley model behind Table 5-1 of the standard ([Jones et al., 2025](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/407074832/1-s2.0-S036013232500798X-main.pdf)). The committee set each space's ECAi so that the hourly probability of infection \( P(I) \le 0.1\% \) is achieved 96% of the time, accounting for space volume, occupant density, breathing rates, and vocalization levels (breathing vs. talking vs. loud vocalization, which doubles required ECAi). Why MERV-A 11+ replaced standard MERV ratings in 2025

One of the most important technical rules in ASHRAE Standard 241 — reinforced in Addendum a — is how mechanical air filters earn credit toward equivalent clean airflow ([ASHRAE Standard 241 Addendum a](https://www.ashrae.org/file%20library/technical%20resources/standards%20and%20guidelines/standards%20addenda/241_2023_a_20241021.pdf)). For a filter receiving recirculated airflow \( V_{\text{RC}} \) with a single-pass infectious aerosol reduction efficiency \( \varepsilon_{\text{PR}} \), its equivalent clean airflow contribution is:

\[ V_{\text{ACS}} = \frac{\varepsilon_{\text{PR}}}{100} \times V_{\text{RC}} \]

To receive any credit toward \( V_{\text{ECAi}} \), a mechanical filter must be rated at least MERV-A 11 or higher ([ASHRAE Standard 241 Fact Sheet](https://www.ashrae.org/file%20library/about/government%20affairs/advocacy%20toolkit/virtual%20packet/standard-241-fact-sheet.pdf)). Beginning January 1, 2025, Standard 241 requires MERV-A ratings determined using ANSI/ASHRAE Standard 52.2 Appendix J ([ASHRAE Standard 241 Addendum a](https://www.ashrae.org/file%20library/technical%20resources/standards%20and%20guidelines/standards%20addenda/241_2023_a_20241021.pdf)). Unlike a standard MERV test, the MERV-A test discharges electrostatic media with potassium chloride (KCl) before measuring efficiency. Cheap electrostatic ("electret") filters often test as MERV 13 when brand new in a laboratory but degrade to MERV 7 or MERV 8 performance within weeks as dust neutralizes their static charge. Requiring MERV-A 11+ ensures that the filter's infectious aerosol capture holds up over its real service life.

Technology-agnostic, but strict on safety and proof.Standard 241 does not endorse any specific air-cleaning technology, but it closes the loophole on unverified marketing claims ([ELI/ASHRAE SSPC 241 Presentation](https://www.eli.org/sites/default/files/files-pdf/Kazukiyo%20Kumagai%20PPT_0.pdf)). In-duct UVGI systems must be tested to ANSI/ASHRAE Standard 185.1 or ISO 15714 using MS2 bacteriophage as the challenge organism ([ASHRAE Standard 241 Addendum a](https://www.ashrae.org/file%20library/technical%20resources/standards%20and%20guidelines/standards%20addenda/241_2023_a_20241021.pdf)). Electronic air cleaners, ionizers, and photocatalytic devices must pass Normative Appendix A bioaerosol chamber testing AND emission safety testing for ozone, formaldehyde, and ultrafine particles ([ASHRAE Standard 241 Fact Sheet](https://www.ashrae.org/file%20library/about/government%20affairs/advocacy%20toolkit/virtual%20packet/standard-241-fact-sheet.pdf)). Fibrous mechanical filters (MERV-A 11–16 and HEPA) are exempt from chemical byproduct testing because they add nothing to the airstream.

How to apply Standard 241 in existing homes and commercial buildings

  • Verify baseline outdoor air first: Check damper operation, outdoor air intake rates, and exhaust fans against ASHRAE 62.1 (commercial) or 62.2 (residential). Standard 241 does not allow filtration to replace minimum code outdoor air ([ASHRAE Standard 241 Fact Sheet](https://www.ashrae.org/file%20library/about/government%20affairs/advocacy%20toolkit/virtual%20packet/standard-241-fact-sheet.pdf)).
  • Upgrade central filtration to MERV-A 13 where static pressure allows: Both CDC/NIOSH and ASHRAE recommend MERV 13 or higher where the blower can handle the pressure drop ([CDC/NIOSH Ventilation FAQ](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ventilation/faq/index.html)). Deep 4- to 5-inch media filter cabinets let systems use MERV-A 13 filters with minimal static pressure penalty.
  • Run the blower during occupied hours in IRMM: Recirculated filtration only contributes to \( V_{\text{ECAi}} \) when air is actively moving through the filter. During high-risk respiratory seasons, set commercial or residential blowers to continuous circulation or scheduled occupied fan operation.
  • Use AHAM AC-1 rated HEPA units or upper-room GUV for dense zones: In classrooms (40 CFM/person), gyms (80 CFM/person), or waiting rooms (90 CFM/person), central rooftop units rarely move enough recirculated CFM to hit the ECAi target alone. Supplement those zones with quiet, CADR-verified portable HEPA cleaners or upper-room UVGI fixtures ([CDC/NIOSH Ventilation FAQ](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ventilation/faq/index.html)).
  • Create a Building Readiness Plan: Standard 241 requires documenting each space's existing \( V_{\text{ECAi}} \), filter specifications, damper settings, and maintenance intervals so the building can switch smoothly between normal operation and Infection Risk Management Mode ([ASHRAE News](https://www.ashrae.org/about/news/2023/ashrae-publishes-standard-241-control-of-infectious-aerosols)).
