Smart Vents and Closed Registers: What the Research Says About Room-by-Room Control

With guest rooms sitting empty after the holidays and bills climbing, closing vents or adding battery-powered smart vents sounds like an easy win. The research tells a more complicated story.

Most homes with central heat have one thermostat and one blower feeding every room. Closing the vent in a spare bedroom, or installing app-controlled smart vents that open and close on their own, looks like a way to stop paying to heat rooms no one is using. The idea makes sense, but in most duct systems the physics works against it.

Who this helps: homeowners with hot and cold rooms, anyone considering smart vents, and households with rooms that sit empty much of the year.

What happens when you close a register

A blower is designed to move a certain amount of air through the whole duct system. Closing registers adds resistance, so pressure inside the ducts rises. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study by Iain Walker measured this on a lab duct system, then modeled typical California houses. Its finding: closing registers “led to increased energy use for a typical California house over a wide combination of climate, duct leakage and number of closed registers.” The savings from conditioning fewer rooms were offset by more duct leakage, because higher pressure pushes more air out of leaky joints into attics and crawlspaces.

The study found two more practical points:

  • Closing registers near the air handler raises pressure and leakage for the whole system. Closing the farthest ones mainly affects only that branch.
  • Closing more than 60% of registers is not recommended. Airflow drops so much that furnaces can run on the high-limit safety switch and air conditioning coils can freeze.

A U.S. Department of Energy review of residential HVAC research summarized the same study this way: closing registers “generally does not save energy, unless the ducts are very tight and the envelope is fairly porous.”

Do smart vents change the math?

Smart vents add sensors and motors, but they still work by closing registers. In a review for a utility efficiency program, Slipstream found that many suppliers cite 20–40% savings on heating and cooling energy. Its preliminary calculations suggest that current whole-home smart vent systems “may not result in more than 5% savings,” below a 10% figure it found in the literature. One reason: rooms rarely drift more than 5°F from the thermostat setting, and heat moves through interior walls and floors from occupied rooms into empty ones anyway.

A Center for Energy and Environment report for the Minnesota Department of Commerce reviewed zoning research and found the same pattern. In one cited lab test, smart damper systems sometimes caused larger duct losses and lower airflow, and significant static pressure adjustments were needed before savings could be realized.

Key takeaway: smart vents can help with comfort, like evening out a hot upstairs bedroom, but they are not a reliable way to save energy on a typical single-speed system. If you use them, close as few as possible, start with rooms farthest from the furnace, and have a technician check airflow and static pressure afterward.

When room-by-room control works better

Slipstream ranked smart vents by how much they can control. Vents that only respond to one room’s temperature have the least potential. Systems that control both the vents and the heating and cooling equipment do better. Vents working together with multi-stage or variable-speed equipment have the most. That’s because the equipment can turn its output down when zones close instead of forcing the same airflow through fewer registers.

Better first steps

For uneven rooms, start with the cause. Leaky or undersized ducts, a dirty filter, missing return air paths, and poor balancing are common culprits. Sealing ducts outside the conditioned space and balancing airflow room by room often fixes comfort complaints that smart vents only mask. Keep supply and return grilles clear of rugs and furniture.

Limitations

The LBNL study modeled California houses with ducts outside the conditioned space. Homes with tight ducts inside the living space may see smaller penalties. The Slipstream figures are preliminary estimates, and smart vent products continue to change.
