Is Your Fireplace Heating the House or Cooling It? Heat Loss, Dampers, and Backdraft Risk

Holiday weekends bring the first fires of the season. An open fireplace looks warm, but building science says it can pull more heat out of the house than it puts in, and change the air pressure your other appliances depend on.

A crackling fire feels like the warmest spot in the house. But a traditional open wood-burning fireplace is mostly an exhaust system. It pulls large amounts of heated room air up the chimney, and outdoor air leaks in through every gap to replace it. That affects your heating bill, the comfort of other rooms, and sometimes the safety of other appliances that vent up a chimney.

Who this helps: homeowners with a wood-burning fireplace, anyone noticing drafts or a smoky smell near the hearth, and homes with older atmospheric gas or oil appliances.

Why an open fireplace loses heat

According to the U.S. EPA, “most traditional open fireplaces lose over 90 percent of the fire’s heat out the chimney, and much of the heated air in the room goes with it.” A separate EPA efficiency page adds that fireplace drafts can make other rooms cooler, and that if central heat is running during a fire, the heater “will work harder” to keep temperatures steady.

The Minnesota Department of Commerce goes further. It says an old wood-burning fireplace “likely loses more heat from your home than it gives off,” especially as the fire dies down. At that stage, more heat is drawn up the chimney than the fire creates, and the weaker airflow “can lead to backdrafting of dangerous flue gasses and smoke into the living space.”

The other 95% of the time: the damper

Most fireplaces sit unused most of the winter, and that’s where a lot of steady heat loss happens. Warm air rises, so an open or poorly sealed damper works like a chimney-sized air leak around the clock. The U.S. Department of Energy Energy Saver guide tells homeowners to check that fireplace dampers close properly. It also suggests an inflatable chimney balloon for flues that aren’t in use, because metal dampers can warp over time and leak air.

Minnesota Commerce offers a simple test: close the damper, light a small piece of paper, and watch the smoke. If it goes quickly up the flue, the damper leaks. Its suggested fixes include sealing around the damper assembly, installing tight-fitting glass doors or an airtight insert, using a chimney balloon in seldom-used fireplaces, and sealing where the chimney meets the wall or ceiling.

Pressure: how a fireplace affects other appliances

A burning fireplace removes air from the house, and so do bath fans, range hoods, and clothes dryers. Together they lower indoor pressure. A literature review of 50 studies from Canada, Europe, and the U.S. found that exhaust fans running at the same time as fireplaces depressurized houses by 3 to 8 pascals on average. That can be enough to work against the natural draft of older atmospheric water heaters and furnaces, which can lead to backdrafting and spillage of combustion gases into the home. Tighter homes are more sensitive to this, because there are fewer leaks to let replacement air in.

Key takeaway: keep the damper fully open during a fire and until it is completely out. Close it tightly once it’s out and cool. Avoid running a big range hood or several exhaust fans while a fire is burning, and keep working carbon monoxide alarms on every level of the home.

Burning more cleanly

If you do use the fireplace, the EPA’s best wood-burning practices call for dry, seasoned wood and keeping appliance doors closed except when loading or stoking a live fire. EPA’s efficiency page also notes that fireplaces create about 20 times the air pollution of EPA-certified wood heaters.

Limitations

Results vary widely with chimney height, house tightness, the damper’s condition, and how the fire is run. The depressurization figures are averages from older studies, and many homes now have sealed-combustion equipment that is far less sensitive to house pressure. Annual chimney inspection remains standard practice.
