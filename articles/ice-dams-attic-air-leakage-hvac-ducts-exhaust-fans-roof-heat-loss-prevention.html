Ice Dams Start Inside the House: Attic Air Leaks, HVAC Ducts, and Exhaust Fans That Melt Roof Snow

Ice dams look like a roof or gutter problem, but building scientists trace most of them to heat escaping from the house below. Attic ductwork, air handlers, and exhaust fans are common sources, which makes this an HVAC issue as much as a roofing one.

After a snowfall, look at the roofs on your street. Some stay evenly white for days. Others show bare stripes, melted patches, and icicles hanging from the gutters. The difference is usually how much heat is escaping into the attic, and where it’s coming from.

Who this helps: homeowners with icicles, ceiling stains, or wet insulation near exterior walls, and anyone whose furnace, air handler, or ductwork sits in an attic.

How ice dams form

Building Science Corporation explains the cause simply: the upper part of the roof is warmer than the lower part. Snow melts over the warm area, the water runs down to the cold eave overhang, and it refreezes. Snow itself is insulation, so deeper snow traps more heat at the roof surface and makes melting more likely, even on very cold days. Once the dam builds up, water can back up under shingles and leak into walls and ceilings.

The HVAC connection

the same digest names pressurized ducts as a direct heat source. Leaky supply ducts in an attic push warm air into the attic, bypassing the insulation, and any ductwork above the insulation acts as a heater. It also notes that foil-faced R-6 duct insulation is not enough in cold climates. University of Minnesota Extension adds that leaky or poorly insulated attic ducts are a heat source, and that kitchen or bath exhaust that ends just above the roof can melt snow.

The DOE Building America Solution Center calls air leakage into the attic one of the biggest causes of ice dams. Common leak points include:

  • furnace flues and chimneys;
  • exhaust fan housings;
  • recessed lights;
  • attic hatches;
  • top plates;
  • kneewalls.

Common heat sources and fixes

Heat sourceWhat to look forUsual fix
Leaky supply ducts or plenum in atticDisconnected joints, unsealed boots, crushed ductSeal joints and boots with mastic, then insulate and bury where practical
Air handler or furnace in atticCabinet leaks, unsealed return, warm air around unitSeal cabinet and connections, insulate, and consider moving the equipment during replacement
Bath or kitchen exhaustFan venting into attic or ending just above roofDuct it outside through a proper roof or wall cap, with a sealed housing
Flue and chimney chasesOpen gap around flueSeal with fire-rated materials and keep required clearances
Ceiling penetrationsDirty insulation (a sign of airflow), open top platesAir seal first, then add insulation

Order matters: the same digest warns that ventilation cannot carry away major air leaks, and that adding vents before sealing can make things worse. Seal the ceiling first, insulate second, and ventilate to remove what little heat remains.

What code and guidance say

Where a jurisdiction has a history of ice at the eaves, International Residential Code Section R905.1.2 requires an ice barrier, either two layers of underlayment cemented together or a self-adhering membrane. It must run from the roof edge to at least 24 inches inside the exterior wall line. That barrier limits leaks behind a dam, but it doesn’t stop the dam from forming. ENERGY STAR says not to cover soffit vents with insulation and to use rafter vents to keep air moving at the eaves. UMass Building and Construction Technology recommends at least R-38 ceiling insulation in northern homes. According to EPA, air sealing and insulating can save an average of 15% on heating and cooling costs.

Safe short-term steps

UMN Extension says a roof rake can remove snow but may damage roofing, and that anyone working on a winter roof risks injury, so it recommends hiring professionals. It also advises against powered attic ventilators, which can create moisture problems and negative pressure in the home. That pressure can matter for gas appliances that vent naturally. Wait until ceilings and walls are dry before repairing interior damage.

Limitations

Roof shape, valleys, skylights, and local snow loads all change risk, and some ice can form even on well-built homes. Insulation levels in this article come from the sources cited. Your local energy code sets the requirement for new work. Fire-rated sealing around flues, and any change to combustion venting, should be done by a qualified contractor.
