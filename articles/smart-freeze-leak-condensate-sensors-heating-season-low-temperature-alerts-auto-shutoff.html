Smart Freeze, Leak, and Condensate Sensors: What They Catch This Heating Season and What the Code Already Requires

Water damage and freezing are among the most common and costly home insurance losses, and many of them start at, or are first noticed by, the heating and cooling system. Here is what each type of smart sensor actually catches, what it depends on, and how it fits with the condensate safety device code already requires.

When a furnace quits on a January night, the first thing to fail is often not comfort but the plumbing. A frozen pipe or an overflowing condensate pan can do more damage than the heating repair itself. Smart sensors are now cheap enough to add to most homes, but they don’t all do the same job.

Who this helps: homeowners heading into winter, owners of rentals, second homes, or properties left empty over the holidays, and anyone with an air handler or furnace in an attic or above finished space.

Why water and freezing deserve attention

The Insurance Information Institute reports that water damage and freezing are the second most frequent homeowners claim after wind and hail. About 1.5 claims are filed per 100 insured homes each year, averaging about $15,400 each (2019 to 2023 data). Frozen pipes are even more expensive. According to Carrier Management, one large insurer handled more than 20,000 freeze-related claims from January 2024 through June 2025, with average payments above $30,000.

What code already requires

For condensate, a safety device is the rule, not an upgrade. Under International Mechanical Code Section 307.2.3, when an overflow could damage the building, each cooling coil or fuel-fired appliance that produces condensate needs one of four protections:

  • an auxiliary pan with a separate drain to a place where you’ll notice it;
  • a separate overflow line;
  • an auxiliary pan with a UL 508 water-level device that shuts off the equipment;
  • or a UL 508 water-level device in the drain line or pan.

High-efficiency furnaces make condensate in winter too, so this applies to heating season, not just summer.

A common winter trap: a float switch that trips on a clogged condensate drain will shut the furnace off, by design. In an empty house, that safety shutdown can lead to a freeze-up. A low-temperature alert is what tells you the heat stopped, whatever the reason.

What the evidence shows

The strongest public data comes from insurers, and most of it involves manufacturers. In a 2020 study, LexisNexis Risk Solutions compared 2,306 homes that installed an in-line automatic water shutoff with similar homes nearby. Water claim events fell 96% and claim severity fell 72% in the year after installation. Over the same period, claims rose 10% in homes without the device. Treat this as a correlation rather than a controlled trial, since people who buy these devices may already be more careful. Results also vary by product.

For buildings, the Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety (IBHS) advises a monitoring system that alerts when indoor temperature drops below a preset point. It also recommends an automatic excess-flow valve on the main water line and, for vacant buildings, keeping heat at 55°F or higher so the inside never falls below 40°F. The IBHS winter checklist adds that existing freeze-protection devices and alarms should be tested before cold weather.

Practical setup checklist

  • Set the low-temperature alert well above freezing, so there is time to respond. Send it to more than one phone.
  • Place leak sensors under the air handler or furnace, the water heater, and the boiler, and near any condensate pump.
  • Ask your technician to test the condensate float switch and confirm it is wired to shut the equipment off.
  • Know where your main water shutoff is and check that it turns.
  • Remember that most Wi-Fi sensors go quiet during a power or internet outage. Cellular or battery-backed hubs reduce that gap.
  • When leaving a home empty, follow Ready.gov winter guidance and arrange for someone to check on the property.

Limitations

Sensors alert you or shut things down. They don’t replace insulation, air sealing, or a heating system in good repair. Insurance discounts and claim results vary by insurer and device, and local code adoption and amendments decide exactly which condensate protection is required.
