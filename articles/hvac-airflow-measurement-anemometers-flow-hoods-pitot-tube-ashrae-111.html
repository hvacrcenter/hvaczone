HVAC Airflow Measurement: Anemometers, Flow Hoods, and Pitot Tube Traverses

You cannot balance what you cannot measure. ASHRAE Standard 111 defines the accuracy of every airflow measurement method — from pitot tube traverses at 5-10% to flow hoods that can error 20-30% in residential systems. NIST research confirms that pitot, hot-wire, and vane anemometers agree within 10% when used correctly.

Airflow measurement is the foundation of HVAC testing, adjusting, and balancing (TAB). Without accurate airflow data, you cannot verify that a system delivers its design CFM, confirm equipment performance, or diagnose comfort problems. ASHRAE Standard 111 — the industry's defining document for measurement, testing, and balancing — specifies the accuracy, calibration, and procedures for every primary airflow measurement method ([ASHRAE Standard 111](https://shop.iccsafe.org/media/wysiwyg/material/8950P223-sample.pdf)).

Pitot tube: the reference standard

The pitot tube measures velocity pressure inside a duct by comparing total pressure (facing into the airflow) to static pressure (perpendicular to it). Converting velocity pressure to velocity using \( V = 1096 \times \sqrt{V P / \rho} \), then multiplying by duct area, yields airflow in CFM. ASHRAE Standard 111 states that flow-rate determinations by the pitot tube and manometer combination can range from 5% to 10% error, and qualified technicians can obtain measurements within 5% and 10% accuracy under good field conditions. When suitable traverse conditions do not exist, measurements can exceed ±10% error ([ASHRAE Standard 111](https://shop.iccsafe.org/media/wysiwyg/material/8950P223-sample.pdf)).

The Log-Tchebycheff traverse method is preferred because its point locations account for wall friction and velocity falloff near duct walls. For accurate results, the measurement location should be at least 7.5 duct diameters downstream and 1.5 diameters upstream from any disturbance. A DOE review confirmed that a rotating vane anemometer at a downstream coil face can provide the same accuracy as an in-duct pitot tube traverse — ±7% for velocities from 100 to 1,100 fpm ([DOE/OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/809884)).

Vane and hot-wire anemometers

Rotating vane anemometers measure air velocity by counting rotations of a calibrated propeller. ASHRAE 111 specifies ±5% accuracy above 200 fpm for smooth flow, but warns of ±30% error in nonuniform flow. They are not recommended below 200 fpm (1.0 m/s). Digital vane anemometers have the same accuracy spec but are not recommended below 50 fpm ([ASHRAE Standard 111](https://shop.iccsafe.org/media/wysiwyg/material/8950P223-sample.pdf)).

Hot-wire (thermal) anemometers measure the cooling effect of airflow on a heated sensor. They excel at low velocities where pitot tubes struggle — the velocity pressure at 100 fpm is only 0.0025 in. w.c., too small for reliable manometer readings. ASHRAE 111 specifies ±10% accuracy above 100 fpm. Both vane and hot-wire anemometers must be field-verified against a pitot-tube traverse to establish a correction factor, and both require manufacturer calibration every six months ([ASHRAE Standard 111](https://shop.iccsafe.org/media/wysiwyg/material/8950P223-sample.pdf)).

Flow hoods: fast but potentially inaccurate

Flow hoods (capture hoods) provide direct CFM readings at grilles and diffusers without duct access — making them the fastest method for terminal balancing. But their accuracy depends heavily on the diffuser type, installation geometry, and whether the hood compensates for its own back-pressure. A DOE/LBNL evaluation found that conventional flow hoods can have potential errors of approximately 20% to 30% of measured flow in residential systems, attributed to poor calibration, sensitivity to grille-flow non-uniformities, and flow changes from the hood's added resistance ([DOE/OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/809884)).

Accuracy requirements by application.A DOE study defined minimum accuracy requirements for residential flow hood applications: ±50% for identifying large leaks or disconnected ducts, ±25% for identifying room-to-room pressure imbalances, ±20% for ensuring room load and comfort requirements, ±10% for determining air handler flow for cooling equipment performance estimation, and ±3% for determining duct leakage. Conventional flow hoods meeting only the ±50% threshold are unsuitable for most residential diagnostic work ([DOE/OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/917398)).

NIST cross-validation

A NIST study comparing airflow measurement methods in an office building found that pitot tube, hot-wire anemometer, and vane anemometer measurements were generally within 10% of each other, with precision-based measurement errors below 3%. However, when the same system airflow was measured at two different locations (inside the fan box versus summed from three supply ducts), results differed by 15% to 30% — demonstrating that measurement location and traverse quality matter more than instrument selection ([NIST](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nistir4905.pdf)).

Practical selection guide

  • Commissioning & TAB: Pitot tube traverse for the reference measurement; vane anemometer for coil face readings.
  • Residential duct testing: Active flow hoods (with back-pressure compensation) for register readings; pitot traverse for main duct verification.
  • Low-velocity measurements: Hot-wire anemometer for velocities below 200 fpm where pitot tubes lack signal.
  • Quick balancing: Flow hood for terminal devices, but verify with a pitot traverse on at least one representative register.
  • Design verification: Compare measured CFM to ACCA Manual D design values — CFM = FPM × Area (sq ft) ([ACCA Manual D](https://www.acca.org/standards/technical-manuals/manual-d)).
