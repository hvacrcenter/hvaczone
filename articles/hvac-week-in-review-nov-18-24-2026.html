HVAC Week in Review: November 18 - 24, 2026

Five stories from the past week that affect what goes into new homes, which refrigerants your next system may use, what equipment will cost, and what this winter’s heating bills look like.

This roundup covers HVAC news published November 18 through November 24, 2026. As always, HVAC Zone is a multi-brand dealer, and nothing here favors one manufacturer over another. Company names are left out where they don’t change the story.

1. Heat pumps reach nearly half of new homes in one large dataset

ACHR News reports on a HERS rating software provider’s analysis of more than 2.1 million rated homes from 2019 to mid-2026. In that data, heat pumps went from roughly 28% of new homes in 2022 to nearly 48% in partial-year 2026. In colder climate zone 5, about one-third of roughly 45,000 homes rated in 2026 used a heat pump as the main heating system, up from about one-sixth in 2023. The report cautions that its data covers only homes rated through that company. The article also cites AHRI data showing heat pumps were 44.7% of combined cooling equipment shipments in August 2026, up from 31% in August 2016.

What it means for you: if you’re buying new construction, ask what the heating system is, what outdoor temperature it was sized for, and what backup heat is installed.

2. States weigh refrigerant rules beyond federal law

ACHR News reports that after the midterms, contractor and distributor groups are worried that a patchwork of state rules could force another refrigerant change before equipment, safety standards, and training are ready. New Jersey is among 10 states listed with draft rules or active advocacy campaigns. California, Washington, and New York already have rules on the books. New York’s amended regulations would restrict even the new A2L refrigerants for some products, starting in 2027 for heat pump water heaters, 2030 for VRF and chillers, and 2034 for almost everything else. A distributor group is challenging those rules. Washington adopted code amendments in October allowing, but not requiring, A3 refrigerants in certain equipment starting in May 2027.

What it means for you: today’s A2L systems remain the current standard for new residential equipment. Rules may change in the coming years, so keep your installation paperwork and refrigerant type on file.

3. Manufacturers report higher revenue and signal more price increases

ACHR News reports that major HVAC manufacturers posted higher third-quarter revenue in both residential and commercial sectors, with data centers driving much of the commercial demand. The article says contractors will likely face continued price increases, citing tariffs and high fuel prices. In a separate item, ACHR News reports that the HVAC mergers market is cooling from its 2021–2023 highs, though private equity remains active.

What it means for you: get written quotes with an expiration date, and ask whether equipment pricing is locked when you sign.

4. Heating oil runs far above last year

The U.S. Energy Information Administration reported that the U.S. average residential heating oil price for the week of November 16 was $5.717 a gallon. That is down about 20 cents from the week before but up about $1.95 from a year earlier. In New Jersey, the price was $5.854 a gallon, up about $1.81 from a year ago. Residential propane in New Jersey was $4.117 a gallon.

What it means for you: oil-heated homes have the most to gain from a tune-up, a combustion check, and a sealed and insulated duct or pipe system this season.

5. The winter grid outlook arrives

NERC’s 2026–27 Winter Reliability Assessment found that resources are adequate for normal winter peaks. However, extreme, wide-area cold could cause shortfalls in some areas. Winter peak demand across assessment areas rose 21 GW from last winter. NERC also flagged natural gas fuel supply during prolonged cold as a continuing concern in New England. We covered the details in our winter reliability outlook.

From the field: fuel prices, the grid, and refrigerant rules all point to the same advice. Have your heating system checked before the first deep cold snap, and keep a record of your equipment’s model, refrigerant type, and service history.

Limitations

The new construction data comes from one company’s rated homes and may not reflect national trends. State refrigerant rules are proposals or under legal challenge and could change. Weekly fuel prices change every week and exclude taxes. The manufacturer revenue story gives no specific price forecasts.
